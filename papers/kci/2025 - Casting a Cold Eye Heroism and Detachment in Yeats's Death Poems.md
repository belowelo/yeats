## Abstract

This paper explores W. B. Yeats's distinctive philosophical vision of death. When confronting his own mortality or observing the deaths of others, Yeats often conveys detachment, control, and heroic resolve. Through close readings of poems such as "Under Ben Bulben," "An Irish Airman Foresees His Death," and "Death," this study examines how Yeats's poetic speakers frequently face death with a "cold eye." It also investigates the underlying influences that shaped this attitude: Nietzschean heroism, belief in reincarnation, and Platonic transcendence. Ultimately, Yeats redefines death not as something to fear, but as a gateway to ontological and philosophical insight-offering spiritual strength that transcends death.
