## Abstract

Four years before his death, Yeats wrote to Olivia Shakespeare of his children's contrasting spirits: When George spoke of Michael's preoccupation with Life as Anne's with death she may have subcon sciously remembered that her spirits once spoke of the centric move ment of phase 1 as the of Life and the centric movement of phase 15 (full moon) as the of Death. 1 If his children were to become metaphors for Yeats' double vision, so too were his poems. All of them have, somewhere for the reader to find, siblings of alternating phases. These alternates, while demonstrating the symbolic parallels between Yeats' sexual and poetical creativity, provide answers to the recent critical dissatisfaction with his final achievement, particularly with Lazuli.2 Yeats' great tribute to death and tragic gaiety found therein has its counterpart in his final rendering of Jane. Thus, the Last Poems are echoes of what preceded them, suggesting voices from the beyond as well as voices of poetic experience.  Lapis Lazuli may bear Yeats' kiss of Death but Crazy Jane on the Mountain cries tears down for the fact of mortality. In Lazuli the speaker grows, becoming the hermaphroditic executor of his opus, while in the final Jane poem the speaker is dwarfed by the creative powers of past mythological lovers.
