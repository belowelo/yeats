## Abstract

W. B. Yeats and the Language of Sculpture considers the way sculptural aesthetics infiltrate Yeats's poetry and critical writing. It seeks to move beyond what previous scholars have described as sculptural analogies in Yeats's poems or qualities of sculpture that imbue the poetry: monumentality, solidity, and timelessness. By carefully tracing some significant overlaps between the work of sculptors and that of poets in the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries, Jack Quin's monograph draws on 'competing ideas of sculpture as solid or fluid, durable or durational, abstract or figurative, autonomous or contingent' (6). In examining changing ideas of sculptural properties in poetry and art writing by Yeats, Pound, Eliot, and others, Quin makes clear the profound influences and interconnections of 'inter-arts aesthetics' at a time of revolutionary state formation in Ireland. The first and fifth chapters are the most cohesive and provide contextual bookends to Yeats's poetic and political career. Pointing to sculptural references in art criticism and cultural writing from the 1890s onward, Quin seeks to expand earlier work that locates a 'late sculptural turn' in Yeats's literary oeuvre. He convincingly argues that the art school training Yeats gained at the Dublin Metropolitan School of Art between May 1884 and July 1885, as well as a stint at the Royal Hibernian Academy, profoundly shaped the poet's early 'fascination with sculpture as a practice, a product, and resource for poetry' (19). Yeats tried out and first began writing about the allied goals of sculpture and poetry in conversations with his art school peers Oliver Sheppard and John Hughes, both of whom would go on to become celebrated Irish sculptors. The 'language of sculpture' appears in Yeats's arguments for new national forms and symbols.
