## Abstract

W. B. YEATS received a significant formal art training at the outset of his poetic career. From May 1884 to July 1885 Yeats was enrolled at the Dublin Metropolitan School of Art (DMSA) after which he attended the Royal Hibernian Academy (RHA).1 The young poet's ongoing engagement with his visual art training can be traced through records of his frequent visits to The National Art Gallery in Dublin beginning on 12 August 1884 and continuing, sometimes sporadically and sometimes almost on a daily basis, up until 31 May 1886.2 From the age of eighteen until he was twenty-one years old, Yeats actively pursued a visual art education in Dublin. The following outlines elements of that visual training. When Yeats enrolled with the DMSA in 1884 he entered a British school of art. Formal art education in Britain came about partly as a consequence of the growing opulence and power of the empire; a power fuelled in no small way by manufacturing industry. The success of British manufacturing industry was undermined by inferior standards in British design. The government was 'forced into action by the pressure of foreign competition and the alarm of the manufacturers' and therefore a system of Art education was developed in order to respond to emerging design needs.3 The Select Committee on Arts and Manufacturers was set up in 1835.4 In general, education in the arts was to be extended from the privileged few engaged in fine arts to the wider members of the industrial community engaged in manufacture. The Royal College of Art, a number of municipal art schools and the Victoria and Albert Museum were established. While the museum would house 'High Art' the schools and colleges would foster technical achievement. The German Gewerbe-Institut system was adopted in the schools; a system that was closely aligned to science and technology.5 Twenty-two schools were created or subsidized between 1842 and 1852 and among their number were Cork and Dublin. The Irish schools were expected to conform to the parent model.
