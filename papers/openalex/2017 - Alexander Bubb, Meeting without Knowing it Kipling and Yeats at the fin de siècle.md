## Abstract

ALEXANDER BUBB'S innovative handling of the lives and work of Rudyard Kipling and W. B. Yeats looks set to blaze a trail in literary biography. The boldness of Bubb's project lies in the idea (inspired by Foucault) of authors circling each other, criticizing and invalidating each other's work, but never actually meeting. Not only did Kipling and Yeats belong to common literary circles and have mutual mentors and friends, but at certain periods they even lived in close physical proximity in London, yet they never met in person. The 1913 cartoon on Bubb's front cover depicts both men at a meeting of the Academic Committee of English Letters, but this is Max Beerbohm's invention: Kipling had declined membership, and was not present. Despite this distance between the two writers, Bubb traces and explores their 'subterranean dialogues', thereby adducing their 'unintentional collaboration' and 'unacknowledged rivalry' (13). One of the identifying features of the fin de siècle is its cultural synergism, with different discursive formations constantly cross-fertilizing each other. Michel Foucault's Archaeology of Knowledge (1972) proposes a methodology necessitating that each form of knowledge is situated in relation to those that are contemporary with it or related to it, producing a 'constellated discourse'. This is the conceptual framework that stimulates and informs Bubb's monograph. The two figures are viewed through shared themes, modes, words, and images. It is a rich, scholarly, and concentrated reading of the two writers' participation in a turbulent, conflicted, and eccentric period.
