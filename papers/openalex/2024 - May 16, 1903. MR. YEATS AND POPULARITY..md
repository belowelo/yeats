## Abstract

It is one of the marks of this strange era that people have to be eccentric in order to do the most obvious and human things. If there be anything right or wrong which you would think that everyone would incline at first sight to say or do, you may be perfectly certain that you will, in this age, find it founded in a Bohemian club or a mad sect. Men gather in queerly-coloured rooms in queerlycoloured clothes and whisper to each other that beauty is more reasonable than ugliness, that the soul exists as well as the body, and that there is not enough room in Cheapside. Meanwhile, all the solid, sensible men in solid, sensible hats walk down Cheapside, their eyes shining with maniacal delusions which ought to make an ox laugh. And it is not in the least a question of which of the views is right. We may be entirely in ignorance of this, and yet see that in the ordinary superficial sense one is odd and the other commonplace. And, as I say, the mystery is that the commonplace beliefs are held by the odd people, and the odd beliefs by the commonplace people. An instance taken at random is the question of vaccination. I know nothing whatever about it, and neither have nor desire to have any opinion, one way or the other. But merely looking at vaccination with the blank observant ignorance of a child looking on at some strange rite, I find it perfectly obvious that vaccination in itself is an astoundingly eccentric thing. The idea of giving a baby a disease mildly on purpose opens to the merely artistic sense a vista of Gilbertian possibilities: mothers complaining that Tommy has not had his mumps this morning, children taking their cholera under a bribe of jam, and parents keeping a herd of bacilli instead of chickens. Considered merely with the first eyes of commonsense, that is to say, it looks as eccentric to see parents vaccinating babies as to see them pulling their teeth out or breaking their legs.
