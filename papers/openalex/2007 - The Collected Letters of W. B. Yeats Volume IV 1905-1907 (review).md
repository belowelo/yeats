## Abstract

Reviewed by: The Collected Letters of W. B. Yeats: Volume IV 1905–1907 George Bornstein The Collected Letters of W. B. Yeats: Volume IV 1905–1907. John Kelly and Ronald Schuchard , eds. Oxford and New York: Oxford University Press, 2005. Pp. lxxvii + 1111. $175.00 (cloth). This fourth volume of the ongoing Collected Letters of W. B. Yeats project under the general editorship of John Kelly lives up to the high standards set by the first three volumes. As with the three previous volumes, the new arrival features a detailed chronology of Yeats's life (with even more detail for the years covered by this particular volume), a bibliographical and historical appendix this time largely connected with the Irish National Theatre Society and the Abbey Theatre, and the usual editorial apparatus, along with letters missed in the earlier volumes. The book totals 1111 pages, along with another 77 of introductory material, weighs over three pounds, covers only three years of Yeats letters, and sells at a price of $175. As Dryden observed of Chaucer, "Here is God's plenty," and therein lies both the achievement and the increasingly obvious problem of this monumental ongoing edition. It is a pleasure to praise the achievement. The years 1905–07 were crucial ones for Yeats (though which years were not?). In the public sphere he threw himself into the theater movement, absorbed both in internal tensions in the evolving National Theatre Society and then as codirector of the Abbey with Lady Gregory and John Synge managing both internal hostilities among the personnel and external ones from overzealous Irish nationalists. Chief among the external wars were the celebrated riots over The Playboy of the Western World in 1907. During these years Yeats published little verse but worked assiduously on writing, revising, and staging some of his most important verse dramas, such as On Baile's Strand or The Unicorn From the [End Page 156] Stars. Ever eager to mold his own canon, he shaped the important eight-volume edition of his Collected Works published during the fall of 1908. Personal upheavals erupted at the same time, though Yeats's personal and public sides often overlapped, as with the Abbey riots. The volume opens with Maud Gonne's divorce petition and follows through the stormy negotiation of the eventual separation agreement with the abusive but politically popular John McBride. Yeats's nationalist mentor John O'Leary died in March of 1907. Yeats chose not to attend the funeral, but the next month did leave from London to join Lady Gregory and her son Robert for an eventful trip to Italy. The visits to cities like Florence, Venice, Ravenna, and Urbino enriched Yeats's poetry and prose for years to come. Besides new information on many details of Yeats's involvement in personal and public events, the letters also record both the evolution of his views and some fine formulations of them. As the editors indicate, Yeats begins in a more optimistic mood that turns gradually more somber but also more determined as difficulties mount, whether with Maud Gonne or with the theater movement. The many discussions of Gonne show Yeats holding views on women far different from those sometimes attached to him by hostile critics. For instance, Yeats defended Gonne vigorously in the face of a patriarchal Ireland that condemned her separation from McBride on grounds of both drunkenness and abuse and that instead wanted her to return to the troubled marriage. "The trouble with these men is that in their eyes a woman has no rights," wrote Yeats to Lady Gregory (47). The introduction, appendix, and annotation are lavish. The editors expertly track down allusions and circumstances, both of which they clarify at length. Researchers will be grateful for the enormous editorial patience and energy on display here but more general modernists and readers of all sorts may feel that the apparatus sometimes overwhelms the text. For example, the first page of letters offers only four lines of very readable text and twenty-six of harder-to-decipher annotation in much smaller font size, and the next presents twelve lines of text but thirty-two lines of annotation.
