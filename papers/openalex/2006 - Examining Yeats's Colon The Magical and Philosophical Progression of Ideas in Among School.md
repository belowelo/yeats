## Abstract

Yeats's Among School Children is perhaps the most anthologized of his poems, undoubtedly because it is narrative in nature, captures a cross-section of both his exoteric and esoteric ideas, and contains a number of intertextual references his other poems that are obvious even casual readers (as opposed Yeats scholars). I myself use it, along with Leda and the Swan and Dialogue of Self and Soul, in many of my literature surveys for these very reasons. Further, the standard interpretation best summarized in Sandra Gilbert's The Poetry of William Butler Yeats--that beneath the seeming discontinuity of child and old man there is always a single whole (73)--is fairly easy for even half-awake students in 8:00 AM humanities general education classes seize upon. Nevertheless, despite the fact that most readers--even experienced critics like Gilbert--tend focus on the fundamental statements and questions that Yeats poses in stanza 8, these are in no way central the poem. They do not connect well with the progression of ideas, and the sudden, strident shift in tone is abrupt and jarring. The two ultimate questions, Are you the leaf, the blossom or the bole? and How can we know the dancer from the dance?, are so obviously rhetorical that they negate any sense of closure whatsoever. In fact, they are almost philosophically clichEd, given the fact that the self-same questions are raised by St. Paul in 1 Corinthians 12-26, in Plato's Parmenides, and in Wittgenstein's Tractatus Logico-Philosophicus. In short, Yeats' s final questions function as feel good statements that are more for the reader's comfort than the poet's. They are simply another attempt to smile on all that smile, and show / There is a comfortable kind of old scarecrow. Among School Children certainly contains many points for scholarly discussion beyond lines 61-64, and the poem is almost certainly about something more serious than the poet's feelings concerning the inevitable march of age--especially given the context of the other poems in The Tower. For example, at the start it is an overtly left-handed evaluation of the educational system in Ireland. It is a Yeatsian adaptation of Swift's Battle of the Books. It reveals Yeats's continuing infatuation for Maude Gonne, despite nine years of marriage Georgie Hyde-Lees. It betrays a bit of anti-Catholic sentiment in his rather offensive use of the verb worship in line 49, and it displays a fondness for Platonic and Neoplatonic philosophy that seems coincide with his purchase of MacKenna's Plotinus in the Spring of 1926 and the beginning of his revisions on A Vision (1925). Still, questions of intentionality aside, most of these topics are only tangentially related the poem in its entirety. Analysis based upon any one of them---or upon the oversimplified questions raised in the last stanza--cannot encapsulate what the poem is about as a whole. In order come grips with its implicit philosophical and magical dilemma, readers need a way ascertain and connect Yeats's own goals concretely, in both youth and age, determine why the dreamy tone of melancholy and discontent that begins emerge in stanza 2 and peaks in line 56--0 self-born mockers of man's enterprise--abruptly disappears in a flurry of nonsequitors that have the same unsatisfactory, discussion-ending effect as the Soul's final statement in Dialogue of Self and Soul: But when I think of [the state of being after death] my tongue's a stone (40). True form, Yeats distracts readers with a number of obvious questions while raising other, more important issues that look like blank statements; for example, the main problems in Among School Children revolve topically around the un stated question in line 56: What is man's enterprise--or what should it be? Given the opening line of the following stanza, the answer seems be connected logically with metaphysical ideas (in the literary sense), and follow in some fashion from the poet's use of complex philosophical allusions in stanzas 2, 5, and 6. …
