## Abstract

With the exception of Player Queen, Resurrection, Calvary, and the two Oedipus adaptations, all of plays appear to take place in Ireland. In some cases, as with Dreaming of the Bones where true Yeatsian may actually trace each stop along the Young Man and two ghosts' journey up the hill to Corcomroe Abbey in County Clare, with three natural stops along the way, the real setting adds immeasurably to the meaning of the play. And certainly, Yeats's reworking of elements from Samuel Ferguson's poem Congal places the action of Herne's Egg in Ireland. So do the set directions for scene 3, as Congal and his entourage gather Before the gates of Tara (Variorum Plays 1022). That much seems clear. uses Irish legends, Irish characters, and Irish place names. And yet, in his 1958 landmark treatment of the in W. B. and Tradition, F. A. C. Wilson casts the shadow of doubt on the full-blooded Irish heritage of the work when he takes closer look at the author's sources. He quotes first from Yeats's introduction to the Selected Poems of Dorothy Wellesley, later printed in Letters to Dorothy Wellesley: Shri Purhoit Swami is with me, and the is his philosophy in fable, or mine confirmed by (Wilson 95, Wellesley Letters 25). Wilson argues: One cannot say that the Irish myth provides with complete source for his narrative, but at least it gives him justification for choosing an Irish setting for the play (103). He makes case for such other influences as Upanishads, Shakespeare's Julius Caesar, Balzac's Seraphita, Alexandra David-Ned's With Mystics and Magicians in Tibet, and Tibetan Book of the Dead (95-136). Wilson also claims that: Yeats translates the 'swan of Bramah' into an Irish heron in deference to the guiding principle of his play; the exotic has to be subordinated to the Celtic (113). Yeats, Wilson writes, had come to see primitive Ireland and India as complimentary (99). Others love it and see Herne's Egg as part of trio of last plays that demonstrate most positively his mastery over theatre crafts. Whether the action takes place in the real Ireland or in an imaginary world that's kind of Yeatsian Yaknapawtapha County may even seem irrelevant at first. After two unsuccessful attempts to stage the properly, I've come to believe an understanding of the difference between real Ireland and Yeats's imaginary one provides the key to proper staging of one of his most challenging, but ultimately rewarding works. Critical opinion of Herne's Egg ranges from downright dismissal to near ecstatic exaltation. On the negative side, Helen Vendler, in s Vision and Later Plays, damns it as a rather arid and contrived piece of theatrical writing. Then, with series of parenthetical blows to the head, she reduces the effort to the role of critical stalking horse: The interesting thing about Herne's Egg is not its humanity (it has very little) nor its stage craft (bad as usual) nor its language (apart from scattered speeches); it's interesting as criticism on King of the Great Clock Tower and A Full Moon in March. She takes Wilson on directly: Mr. Wilson speaks of the various scenes and speeches as 'moving,' 'exciting,' 'magnificent,' 'splendid,' 'beautifully modulated,' 'vivid,' 'beautiful and pregnant,' and so on. But the Herne's Egg is none of these things (160). question of sources, Irish or otherwise, remains non-issue with Vendler. Yeats's attempt at philosophical farce in which seven soldiers rape priestess does not fit her notion of the man himself.
