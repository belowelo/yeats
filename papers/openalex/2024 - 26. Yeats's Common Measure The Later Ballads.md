## Abstract

This chapter considers Yeats's appropriations of orality and its meaning before different audiences and readerships. In particular, it focuses on the political ramifications of Yeats's oral aesthetics. Yeats's 1930s ballads seem to offer a deliberate roughening up of his style to enable the unpredictable and the unforeseen. In their original printings they also play uncomfortably with conservative ideologies such as fascism and feudalism. Ultimately, though, the chapter concludes they are too idiosyncratic and wilfully troublesome to be straightforwardly subsumed to traditionalist or right-wing ideologies. Rather, they establish antinomies between the heterogenous and violent energies of the folk from which populist forms have come, and the knowledge required by their informed audience. They operate in a paradoxical, rancorous realm of decidedly impure and synthetic populism and authenticity, one marked by terror and gaiety.
