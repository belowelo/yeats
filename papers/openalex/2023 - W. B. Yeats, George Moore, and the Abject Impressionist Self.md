## Abstract

W. B. Yeats, George Moore, and the Abject Impressionist Self Tom Walker (bio) I In 1911, George Moore published Ave, the first volume of what was to become Hail and Farewell (1911-1914), his three-part memoir of the Irish Literary Revival. It contains an account of meeting W. B. Yeats in Stephen's Green in Dublin in 1899, amid the turmoil surrounding the staging of Yeats's play The Countess Cathleen. Moore was in a "sylvan" mood, captivated by the park's natural delights: By the water's edge we met a willing duck pursued by two drakes-a lover and a moralist. In my good nature I intervened, for the lover was being hustled off again and again, but mistaking the moralist for the lover, I drove the lover away, and left the moralist, who feeling that he could not give the duck the explanation expected from him, looked extremely vexed and embarrassed. And this little incident seemed to me full of human nature … "Yeats's thoughts," however, "were far above nature that morning, and he refused to listen." Moore cries in response at Yeats to awaken and see the springtime, "poking him" with the observation that: "Your best poems came to you through your eyes."1 This vignette might seem to encapsulate these writers' contrasting attitudes towards impressionism. As Adam Parkes observes, Moore was not only "one of impressionism's preeminent literary practitioners," but also a champion of impressionist painting via his extensive art criticism.2 [End Page 274] This draws on Moore's experiences living and, for a time, studying art in Paris during the 1870s. There he became acquainted with several painters, most notably Édouard Manet, who painted Moore's portrait several times and remained an aesthetic lodestar throughout Moore's career. Indeed, Moore's Modern Painting (1893) praises Manet's achievement in terms that distinctly echo his advice to Yeats. It describes, after the "slight adventure into symbolism" of Olympia, how "Manet's eyes were closed to all but the visible world. … Never before was a great man's mind so strictly limited to the range of what his eyes saw."3 Parkes suggests an impressionist style had been transferred to Moore's fiction by the publication of Esther Waters (1894), which "employs limited point of view to create effects of immediate, untutored perception."4 Richard Allen Cave points to a later stylistic shift in the early 1900s, under the influence of Moore's then-lover Clara Christian, who had been a pupil at the Slade "of the first generation of English Painters to respond to Impressionist principles." Through her, "Moore came to appreciate what he had not seen till now: that the precepts behind such art could be applied creatively to prose fiction." Cave argues that The Lake (1905) in both style and subject matter reads like an extension of the earlier appraisal of Manet, with sanity lying in "a glad worship of the visible world."5 By contrast, Yeats repeatedly cast himself in antipathy to impressionist painting such that Rob Harris, with some justification, can suggest that "Yeats did not dislike Impressionism, he loathed it."6 In 1905, Yeats put his name to a joint letter to the Irish Times in support of Hugh Lane's attempt to raise a subscription to keep a collection of mainly French pictures in Dublin, to form the basis for a modern art gallery. The letter highlighted that the collection included "two great pictures by Manet," Le Concert aux Tuileries (1860) and the portrait of Mademoiselle Éva Gonzalès (1870), and also suggested that the works by Monet and Manet were particularly "essential to a study of modern art."7 Thereafter, however, Yeats started to reckon with his discomfort with impressionism and especially the work of Manet.
