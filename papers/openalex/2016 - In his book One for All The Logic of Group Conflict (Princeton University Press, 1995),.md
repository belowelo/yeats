## Abstract

Hardin recruits Yeats' verse to support his view that ethnic conflict often develops from community-based ignorance and exclusionary norms, factors that fuel the intense and violent actions of the 'worst'. The 'best', the edu cated and urbane, are freed from the epistemological fetters and the con straining force of exclusionary norms that pervade many communities. As Hardin notes in the lines immediately following those of Yeats: 'Many of the convictions that the best lack are largely perverse convictions artificially made seemingly right by their grounding in a particular community, espe cially an ignorant one' (p. 139). Hardin's use of Yeats is ironic. The life of William Butler Yeats, in terms of both his poetry and his politics, could be used as a counter-example to many of Hardin's own assumptions and methods in explaining ethnic/nationalist conflict. In fact, this commentary will do just that. This article proceeds in three parts. First, I will summarize Hardin's approach to ethnic conflict concentrating on constituent elements and underlying assumptions that are common to many political science treatments of ethnic conflict. Secondly, I will briefly discuss the political life and ideas of Yeats, concentrating on his vacillating communal identities. In many ways, Yeats is an ideal subject for a study of nationalist/ethnic politics. With a life stretch ing from 1865 to 1939, Yeats witnessed several eras of Irish nationalist politics and even served as a Senator after the birth of the Irish Free State. At times, he was an ardent Irish nationalist, at other times, he held Irish nationalists in aristocratic contempt. In effect, Yeats provides us a case study in fluctuation of identification. Because of his prolific writings, including his prose and letters, we are left with a record of his thought. Because of his poetry, we are left with a record of his emotions, an aspect that will play a large part in this article. Finally, I will use the life and writings of Yeats to illustrate several potential shortcomings in Hardin's, and by implicit exten sion, many other social scientists' approach to ethnic conflict.
