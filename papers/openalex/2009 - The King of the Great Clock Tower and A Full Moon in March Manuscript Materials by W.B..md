## Abstract

Reviewed by: The King of the Great Clock Tower and A Full Moon in March: Manuscript Materials by W.B. Yeats Giuseppe Serpillo Richard Allen Cave , ed. The King of the Great Clock Tower and A Full Moon in March: Manuscript Materials by W.B. Yeats. Ithaca and London: Cornell University Press, 2007. Pp. lxxiii + 374, illustrated. $85.00 (Hb). When, early in November 1933, Yeats sent Olivia Shakespear two stanzas of what could be the opening song of a new dance play, he scarcely knew how or whether it would develop at all. What emerged after long and frantic drafting and redrafting was the first prose version of The King of the Great Clock Tower. However, even before it had been staged, he felt there was something wrong with the play. The three main characters – the King, the Queen, and the Stroller – did not quite match. The Stroller was at times dangerously close to farce; the Queen had not acquired sufficient psychological and emotional subtlety; and the King – well, if there was a character too many, it was the King, whose personality did not emerge from his language and whose motivations were not fully clear. Besides, [End Page 240] Yeats was annoyed by Ezra Pound's response to the play, which he had dismissed as "putrid," but in particular by his remark that his language was "Nobody language." At this point, Yeats wanted to prove, especially to himself, that he was not too old to write poetry. Redrafting the prose play to produce a verse play, Yeats solved some of the problems he had been confronted by in the first version. He dropped all attempts at aligning the King with a fitting figure from history or myth – such as O'Rourke, Dervorgilla's husband in the story of her adulterous relationship with MacMurrogh – as this would have restricted the play to the confines of Irish history. Also, the two male Attendants, who, as in Yeats's plays for dancers, fold and unfold the cloth that hides or discovers the characters, were now distinguished as to vocal range: one bass, one tenor; and the King and the Stroller were distinguished, not just for their different roles, but through rhythm and syntax. As for the Queen, she had decidedly become "A screen between the living and the dead," which, of course, was her role from the very beginning – only it was left mostly to Ninette de Valois's choreography skilfully to suggest the supernatural powers of a woman poised between the two dimensions. By drafting and redrafting the prose play to produce a verse play, Yeats ended up with a completely new play, which he called A Full Moon in March; like his previous plays for dancers, it was a chamber play for performance in a small space rather than in a proper theatre. Yeats never saw it staged: the first professional performance took place only in 1950. The new play was at first called The Great Clock Tower, then it became The Severed Head; it went through two more titles, The Swineherd and The Lover's Night, before taking its final title of A Full Moon in March. As will be immediately clear to all Yeats readers, it recalls Phase Fifteen of the twenty-eight incarnations in his symbolic "Great Wheel," which, as he writes in A Vision, is a phase of complete beauty. In this last version of the play, the King disappears and the relationship between the Stroller and the Queen is definitely that of lovers beyond the constrictions of time and space: a mixture of the metaphysical and the erotic. The Stroller, transfigured by the power of love, has completely changed from the boorish lout of the first prose play and has become a poet. The kissing of his severed head by the Queen is miles apart from Salome's kissing of Jokanaan's head in Wilde's play, allowing Yeats to dodge the possible charge of plagiarism he had tried to shrug off since the initial prose version of The King of the Great Clock Tower.
