However, Mr O'Brien rehabilitates Yeats in order to claim most em phatically his own belief in the poet's Fascism. He manifests a lack of sympathy with the Yeatsian 'forma mentis', and a lack of consideration of what has gone before in the Yeatsian canon. Without becoming impas sioned about Fascism and Nazism, it must be possible, I believe, neither to apologize nor to vindicate but rather to approach Yeats's political ideas with no preconceived theories and with a desire to understand and to set them squarely in their proper historical setting. This article is intended as such an approach. I wish furthermore to indicate Yeats's interest in Jonathan Swift and above all in Giambattista Vico, the eighteenth-century Italian philosopher; this interest and the study which ensued were, in my opinion, responsible for the triumphant synthesis of politics with philosophy of history which gives unity and breadth to the vision of the later poems and plays.
