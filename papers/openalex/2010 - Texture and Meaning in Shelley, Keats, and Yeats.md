## Abstract

'TEXTURE' IS A METAPHORICAL TERM (originating in the craft of weaving) that is used in criticism of literature, music, and art with varying degrees of clarity and consistency. In this essay I shall apply it to the 'woven' interrelationship of the 'physical' elements, the verbal sounds, in the line of verse. I start from the conventional distinction of texture from structure, which denotes metrical form (line and stanza type) and includes rhyme-patterns that constitute part of a given form, for example end-rhymes in the heroic couplet or the rhyme royal stanza ('structural rhyme'). Rhyme of various types in other positions, along with assonance, I place under texture, because it is not determined or determining but free. A firm distinction between these types of rhyme has become hard to maintain since the arrival of modernist free verse, such as the type used by Eliot in 'Prufrock', in which the irregularly recurring end-rhymes can't be called strictly 'determined or determining'. But the distinction should be clear enough in the three poets I discuss – including (with appropriate modifications) the latest of them, Yeats. For though writing a hundred years later than Shelley and Keats, Yeats adhered broadly to the norms of traditional English poetry, including structural rhyme: he never wrote non-metrical verse and, outside a few shorter blank-verse pieces, usually semi-dramatic dialogues, only rarely wrote verse that did not use some kind of rhyme. However, since all the pieces I shall consider use end-rhymes as important expressive elements of meaning and emotion, and since they have close relations with the other sound-elements in the line, to exclude them completely from a discussion of texture as I have defined it would be artificial and unhelpful.
