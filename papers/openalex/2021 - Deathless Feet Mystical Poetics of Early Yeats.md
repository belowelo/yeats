## Abstract

This chapter examines Yeats's early mystical orientation, focusing on his foundational engagement with Indian spirituality, both philosophically and poetically understood. The central argument of the chapter is that Yeats's early preoccupation with India remains fundamental to the syncretic spirituality of his thought, blending into the larger literary, cultural, and philosophical enterprises of his life. The first two sections concentrate on the poet's formative associations with the Vedantist-Theosophist Mohini Mohun Chatterjee and the work of Kalidasa. With a view to examining Yeats's creative appropriation of these disparate materials, these sections closely read a discarded 1880s poem on Chatterjee's teaching and its later 1929 version, "Mohini Chatterjee", as well as his early Indian poems, collected in Crossways . These readings are supplemented by critical analysis of the relevant Indian texts, which not only offers fresh insights into the poems concerned but also illuminates the extent of Yeats's imaginative improvisation. In order to show the connection of Yeats's formative Indian orientation to the creative, mystical, and cultural-nationalist activities of his early career, the final section of the chapter briefly dwells upon the relevant aspects of the poet's engagement with symbolism, decadence-aestheticism, and Irish celticism, thereby recontextualising these often-explored aspects of the poet's career. This section also offers suggestions about Yeats's personal faith, a subject the treatment of which is often eschewed in scholarly considerations of his mystical connections.
