## Abstract

Like Walt Whitman, W. B. Yeats contained multitudes, a potpourri of identities and qualities that included poet, ascendant Protestant, husband, father, folklorist, dramatist, senator, producer, mystic, nationalist, astrologist, philanderer, and devoted correspondent with his wife, George. In "In Memory of W. B. Yeats," Auden wrote that Yeats was "silly like us," then acknowledged in the same line that his "gift survived it all." Auden in particular thought Yeats's collaboration in translating the Upanishads with Shri Purohit Swami was a "deplorable spectacle of a grown man occupied with the mumbo-jumbo of magic." Auden's wise acknowledgment of Yeats's silliness "like ours" galvanized his elegy with a frankness that verified Yeats's humanness as an essential alloy to his poetic "gift." It allowed him, despite his silliness, to compose poetry that "flowed on south / From ranches of isolation and the busy griefs, / Raw towns that we believe and die in." A poetry that was, in short, "A way of happening, a mouth." While there have been two biographies of Yeats's life—Richard Ellmann's Yeats, the Man and the Masks and R. F. Foster's exhaustive, two-volume biography, The Apprentice Mage, 1865–1914 (W. B. Yeats: A Life, Vol. I) and W. B. Yeats: A Life, Volume II: The Arch Poet 1915–1939—that explore his multitudes, a comprehensive edition of Yeats's correspondence with his wife, George, has not existed until now.
