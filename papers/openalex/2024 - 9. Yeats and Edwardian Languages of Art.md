## Abstract

This chapter uses a comparison with the conception of art found in the writings of Roger Fry around 1910 to develop an interpretation of how Yeats's views about the visual arts and aesthetics more generally developed up to the midpoint of the second decade of the century. It suggests that the Edwardian languages of art found not only in Fry's writing but also that of other art writers at the time, including Walter Sickert and D. S. MacColl, offer convergencies with Yeats's contemporary thinking. These convergences centre on the emotional effects of art works, the relationship between art and daily life, and the use of a cross-medial sense of form, genre and mode to conceptualise these issues. Also examined are the structures and networks that supported this exchange of ideas. Delineating Yeats's place in a heterogenous Edwardian art world, marked by such critical thinking and debate, underlines that the poet's engagements with the visual arts are not reducible to a false Victorian-to-modern teleology.
