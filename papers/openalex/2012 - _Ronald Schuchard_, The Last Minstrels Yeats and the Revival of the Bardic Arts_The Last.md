Pp. xxvi+447.Mary Helen ThuenteMary Helen ThuenteNorth Carolina State University Search for more articles by this author PDFPDF PLUSFull Text Add to favoritesDownload CitationTrack CitationsPermissionsReprints Share onFacebookTwitterLinked InRedditEmailQR Code SectionsMoreRonald Schuchard's The Last Minstrels: Yeats and the Revival of the Bardic Arts demonstrates the lifelong importance to Yeats's writings of his endeavors to revive the oral tradition of chanting and musical speech and reconstructs what amounts to a lost literary movement central to the development of poetry in the early twentieth century. He demonstrates that what has been generally regarded among Yeats scholars as an eccentric enthusiasm, usually referred to by the title of Yeats's 1902 essay, "Speaking to the Psaltery," was enormously important to his own work and to the development of modern poetry. Using an impressive array of new archival materials, Schuchard provides a detailed and fascinating narrative of Yeats and Florence Farr's twenty-two-year collaboration to take the art of chanting to Great Britain, America, and Europe and reveals for the first time the influence of their auditory poetics on the visual paradigm of the Imagists.From the beginning of his career Yeats was determined to return the living voice of the poet to the center of culture—on its platforms, stages, and streets—thereby establishing a spiritual democracy in the arts for the nonreading as well as the reading public. For years he experimented with Florence Farr in the arts of musical speech, speaking to musical notes and regulated declamation, far different from setting poems to music. To Yeats and Farr, the psaltery was at once an instrument and a symbol of evocation, and the living voice of the poet-reciter an expression of the emotion and ecstasy of vision. Ezra Pound was convinced that when Yeats chanted his poems in a strange, trancelike state in a moment of passionate intensity he achieved "the absolute rhythm" of a poet (52). Yeats's conception and construction of drama was also grounded in musical speech as well as related techniques involving symbolic scenery and rhythmical acting.In the course of ten insightful and meticulously researched chapters of historical, biographical, and critical analysis, Schuchard demonstrates that Yeats's verse-speaking theories were the unifying matrix of his lyric, dramatic, and magical practices. The first chapter, "Bardic Forefathers," sets forth the broad "bardic" context that Schuchard defines as an oral tradition encompassing bards, minstrels, and ballad writers. Yeats believed that chanting was rooted in Greek, Egyptian, and European bardic practices as old as their religions. Chanting in the poetic composition and performance of William Blake, the English Romantic poets, Tennyson, and Swinburne also influenced Yeats. Yeats's efforts to revive the spoken arts of poetry and drama involved a broad range of literary personalities such as Ezra Pound and George Bernard Shaw, literary critics such as Thomas MacDonagh, and even D. H. Lawrence and James Joyce, who was deeply impressed by Farr's chanting of Yeats's dramatic lyrics.A rich array of illustrations, quotes, anecdotes, and personal details about even minor players in Yeats's proselytizing for the spoken arts enliven the fascinating tale that Schuchard reconstructs. One quotation that Schuchard did not include (not surprising given the seriousness of his portrayal of Yeats's attempts to become a modern bard) is Shaw's description of Farr's chanting and musical speech as "a nerve-destroying crooning like the maunderings of an idiot-banshee" (Michael Holroyd, Bernard Shaw, vol. 1, 1856–1898 [New York: Random House, 1988], 208). The cast of characters in The Last Minstrels had lively personal as well as professional relationships. Florence Farr was both Shaw's and Yeats's lover. Schuchard reveals the rich lives and range of intellectual interests of people like Farr, whom Yeats scholars usually consider only in personal and professional relation to Yeats. Figures like music historian and instrument maker Arnold Dolmetsch, whose roles in Yeats's life are briefly noted in Roy Foster's monumental biography (W. B. Yeats: A Life, 2 vols. [Oxford University Press, 1997–2003]), have important roles in Schuchard's larger story of early twentieth-century literature and culture. Schuchard demonstrates, for example, that Dolmetsch's ideas were a major influence on both Yeats and Pound. The impact of Yeats's chanting movement on Vachel Lindsay, who worked out of the American vaudeville tradition, suggests the scope of Yeats and Farr's influence.Some well-known anecdotes about Yeats, such as his seemingly peculiar behavior of chanting as he composed poetry, make more sense when understood in this bardic context. Schuchard provides extensive, often fascinating, background materials. He unearthed newspaper accounts of Yeats and Farr's lectures and performances in England, Scotland, and America to provide a detailed history of how extensive their activities were. Schuchard provides interesting information about the social and literary contexts in which Yeats and Farr worked. At one New York event, Farr encountered Mark Twain, the granddaughter of Mrs. William Astor, and the son of an immigrant from County Carlow who had founded Collier's Publishing Company. When Schuchard mentions that Farr's performances were featured in a 1907 article entitled "Lilting to the Psaltery" in the Saturday Evening Post, he notes that authors publishing in that magazine in 1906–7 included Booth Tarkington, Joseph Conrad, and O. Henry.The major story that Schuchard reveals is how in the first decade of the twentieth century, Yeats and Farr made the art of regulated, rhythmic declamation the most visible poetic movement in Great Britain. Most important, Schuchard's research uncovered how Yeats and Farr sparked the first phase of the Imagist movement in 1909 and set off a dialogue on prosody that energized the development of free verse and Imagism for the next five years. As Yeats and Farr battled the visual paradigm of the Imagists with their auditory poetics and rhythmic principles, they influenced the third proposition of the Imagist manifesto—"As regarding rhythm: to compose in sequence of the musical phrase, not in sequence of a metronome" (257). Schuchard quotes the preface to the second volume of Some Imagist Poets (1916): "Poetry is a spoken and not a written art" (283). An interesting fact left out of an early account in the Egoist of the origins of Imagism, but that Schuchard found in a manuscript, is that many of the early Imagist meetings took place at the Irish Literary Society in London. This is but one of many examples of how Schuchard's extensive research reveals new and important information. He even tracked down Florence Farr's spiritual diary in a private collection and, from a descendant of Pound, obtained his personal copy of Arnold Dolmetsch's Interpretation of the Music of the XVIIth and XVIIIth Centuries (1915) with Pound's marginal notes. Another example of Schuchard's thorough and meticulous scholarship is his discovery that what had been published as a previously unpublished single essay by Yeats is a conflation of three separate entries in a Yeats notebook in the National Library of Ireland.Schuchard also discusses how Yeats and Pound considered the Bengali poet Rabindranath Tagore to be a living exemplar of the modern troubadour and the spoken arts. Schuchard's analysis of the break between Yeats and Pound, and of the relationship between Yeats and T. S. Eliot, demonstrates his extensive knowledge of all three writers and their times. Schuchard's penultimate chapter, "Minstrels and Moderns," reveals the depth and coherence of Yeats's poetics and of his understanding of English literary history and explores how during World War I Yeats's verse-speaking experiments included chanting and other adaptations from Noh drama for his dance plays and choruses. The final chapter explores how the advent of broadcasting inspired Yeats's "last attempt to stem the tide of artificial syntax with impassioned lyrics, lyrics sounding more like poems singing than poems thinking" (334).This original and important book broadens our understanding of early twentieth-century poetry and culture and of important dimensions of Yeats's career—his cultural nationalism, his aims for the Abbey Theatre, his philosophy and techniques of poetic composition, and his dynamic place in a complex of interrelated arts in London and Dublin. The Last Minstrels: Yeats and the Revival of the Bardic Arts is essential to our understanding of modern literature and of W. B. Yeats. This is an impressive capstone work that only a scholar of Schuchard's stature, skills, and background in Yeats, Eliot, and modern literature could have written.
