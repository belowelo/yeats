## Abstract

IN 'THE DISCOVERY OF THE CELT', Holbrook Jackson recognized Yeats as 'the chief figure of the Celtic Renaissance … the fullest expression of the intellectual Celt—poet, mystic, and patriot—expressing himself in an imaginative propaganda which has affected the thoughts and won the appreciation of the English-speaking world.'1 To convey the dominant mood of nineties Celticism, however, Jackson placed at the head of his chapter a quotation from Fiona Macleod which reads in part: Through ages of slow westering, till now we face the sundown seas, we have learned in continual vicissitude that there are secret ways whereon armies cannot march. And this has been given to us, a more ardent longing, a more rapt passion in the things of outward beauty and in the things of spiritual beauty.
