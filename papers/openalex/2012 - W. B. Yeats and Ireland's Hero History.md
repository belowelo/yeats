## Abstract

Toward the end of his life, the 73-year-old Yeats invited the 72-year-old Maud Gonne to tea at Riversdale, his home at the foot of the Dublin mountains. As he rose to greet her, he said, "Maud, we should have gone on with our Castle of the Heroes, we might still do it." Recalling this, their last meeting, Maud Gonne said, "I was so surprised that he remembered, I could not reply."1 The "Castle of Heroes" is only the most literal expression of Yeats's desire to awaken Ireland to its mythic and heroic potential by restoring the ancient Celtic gods of the earth. Working at the intersection of history, invention, and the heroic ideal, Yeats aimed to create a new Irish national imaginary by renewing "eternal and ancient Ireland" and rejecting "that passing and modern Ireland of prosaic cynicism and prosaic rivalries" (UP I, p. 141).2 His code of heroic values radically altered not only the forms of Irish cultural nationalism but also asserted the constitutive power of art to refashion history itself.3 Yeats's heroic aesthetic is closely aligned with his attitude to history, whether in his Shelleyan belief that the "Imagination" operates in opposition to historical imperatives or in his Nietzschean insistence that the hero can transcend history.4 As Yeats himself claimed, "History is necessity until it takes fire in someone's imagination and becomes myth or passion" (EX, p. 336).KeywordsMonumental HistoryHistorical SpaceIrish HistoryIrish LiteratureHeroic FigureThese keywords were added by machine and not by the authors.
