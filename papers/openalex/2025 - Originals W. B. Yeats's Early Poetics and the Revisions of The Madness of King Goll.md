## Abstract

This study examines the five-stage revision of W. B. Yeatsʼs early poem ʻThe Madness of King Gollʼ (1887), demonstrating how late nineteenth-century Irish cultural nationalism and European literary movements shaped its transformation.Although the poem underwent unusually extensive revisions from its first appearance in The Leisure Hour to its final form in the 1899 edition of Poems, it has attracted relatively little critical attention in recent Yeats scholarship.In this work, Yeats explored one of his most enduring artistic concerns: how to retell Irish myth and legend in poetic form.He responded to contemporary literary contexts and to shifting demands of medium and readership by adopting diverse approaches across each version.Based in London during his early career, Yeats carefully negotiated the political and religious sensitivities of British readers while participating in Irish cultural nationalism and establishing his identity as a poet rooted in the Irish oral literary tradition.As he was revising the poem, his engagement with nationalism became more ambivalent, even as he absorbed the influence of French Symbolism and reshaped his poetics accordingly.Originally conceived as a ballad mediating between history and legend, ʻThe Madness of King Gollʼ gradually evolved into a Symbolist poem grounded in Irish imaginative tradition.By tracing this transformation, the study reconsiders the development of Yeatsʼs early poetics in the light of the cultural contexts of the 1880s and 1890s.
