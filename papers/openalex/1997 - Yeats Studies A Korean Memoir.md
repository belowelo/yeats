## Abstract

John Cussen  Yeats Studies: A Korean Memoir  Someday a Korean writer may do for the city of Seoul what James Joyce did for Dublin. In a novel of resounding literary significance, he or she will write up the hometown with such thoroughness that "if the city one day suddenly disappeared from the earth it could be reconstructed out of the book," which is what Joyce claimed to have done in Ulysses. The idea that some talented Korean might make the definitive postmodern big book out of post-Olympic Seoul just as Joyce made the quintessential modernist novel out of post-Victorian Dublin is intriguing. Despite the differences between the cities, the two books are likely to resemble one another—in breadth (Joyce wrote a fat book), in humor (the Seoul book will be funny) and in what they have to say about the perennial conflict between young and old: who should teach whom. I've been thinking about Ulysses because Sunday is Bloomsday, named after Leopold Bloom, the novel's pathetic hero. Annually on this day in Dublin, literary buffs get out in droves to do the Bloomsday Walk. That is, they tour the streets that Bloom zigzagged on June , , the day of the novel's imagined action. The commemoration is an early summer tradition in Ireland, a chance for Dubliners to exult in their city's world-class literary achievement, to sing, drink and talk. I wish I were there. But I'm not. I'm here in Korea, an expatriate resident of Seoul's suburban precincts, feeling far from the literary action. But maybe I shouldn't feel too far removed. Since coming to Seoul, I have had the good fortune to be taken in by a group of Korean university professors who, like myself, take a professional interest in Anglo-Irish literature. They are The Yeats Society of Korea, an academic group whose primary focus is the work of William Butler Yeats. On the last Saturday of May at Ewha University, I attended the society's most recent convocation. The scholarship was impressive . The Korean professors know their Yeats and handle the material ingeniously . I remember best a paper about one of Yeats's surrogate love dalliances —surrogate to his unrequited obsession for Maud Gonne. Some of Yeats's most memorable poetry was written in response to his rejection by Maud. I also remember a paper delivered by a young American-trained scholar. She talked about Yeats's "Ego-Self relation," frozen in the tantalizing moment of the instant before a kiss. Hers was a hard-wrought thesis, which is the way contemporary literary explication is supposed to be worked out. I said to myself , "That young woman is a lot smarter than I am," and decided against engaging her in the discussion session that followed. Not so, my Korean colleagues. Although four presentors were available for interrogation, all but one of a half-dozen questions went to the junior intellectual . Her theses were tested by proven academics on average twenty years her senior and eighty pounds out of her weightclass. Although they spoke in Korean , I surmised that their comments were part homage (new, bright ideas get a reaction), part declaration of attendance (when I was in religion, we called this part of the program "the bishop's remarks"), part academic discourse (small component) and part admonition. The young intellectual's brilliance, it seemed, had indiscreetly surpassed its age-appointed boundaries; in Korea, it must still be important that the old teach the young, and not vice versa. To her credit, the young presentor met her interrogators savvily. She answered as if only the second of the questions' four component dynamics mattered. In his first interview with Yeats, James Joyce did not behave himself nearly so well. The story goes like this. At age twenty, with one slight publication under his belt and a lackluster university record, Joyce determined he was ready to mix it up with the best minds of his country. He first called on the poet George Russell, who found him insufferably sure of himself. For the sake of amusement, Russell passed Joyce on to Yeats, Dublin's most accomplished literary figure.
