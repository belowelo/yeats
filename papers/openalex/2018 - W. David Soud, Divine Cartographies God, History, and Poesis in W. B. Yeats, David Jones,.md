## Abstract

The relationship between literature and theology has been a burgeoning preoccupation of critics in recent decades, pre-eminently so in early modern studies, in which the 'turn to religion' has brought with it historically nuanced accounts of the devotional practices and doctrinal debates that shaped the period's literature. Modernist scholarship has been comparatively slow to embrace this context, but W. David Soud's Divine Cartographies forms part of an emergent body of work on twentieth-century religious culture including Suzanne Hobson's Angels of Modernism (2011) and Pericles Lewis's Religious Experience and the Modernist Novel (2010). A valuable study of the poetics of religion, Soud's monograph patiently details three authors' engagement with theology and productively complicates our understanding of its importance to their life and work. The book's choice of subjects does not herald a departure from the established modernist canon or from those figures within it about whose religious concerns much has previously been written: three chapters focus in turn on W. B. Yeats's interest in Indic traditions alongside his poetry of the 1930s, the influence of David Jones's Roman Catholicism on The Anathemata (1952) and the Anglo-Catholic faith of T. S. Eliot as it finds articulation in Four Quartets (1943). Rather, the originality of Soud's study lies in the array of material he brings to bear on each poet's writings and beliefs. Divine Cartographies examines how this trio responded to what they perceived as the 'pernicious, even dehumanizing development' of secularization while shrewdly demonstrating how their responses contradict this narrative of secularization and testify instead to the 'proliferation of competing moral and spiritual sources' in the early-twentieth century (6–7). By tracing the reception of such movements as liberal Protestantism, Neo-Thomism, and Theosophy, Soud evinces the interplay between these diverse contexts in his subjects' writings from the century's middle decades.
