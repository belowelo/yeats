(2) asserts a similar concept in A Vision (1925), a text he developed at same time he worked on and Swan. As Terence Brown notes, Yeats hoped conflict, dominant subject of his prophetic book, would be catalyst of spiritually invigorating change. (3) poems drafts portray tragic stories of an animal brutality all but destroys divinity of Zeus and violates Leda, even though she emerges as an individual with agency and an identity independent of her violation. final form of poem, read in context of drafts, reaffirms tragic consequences of Leda's rape but also affirms her potential for self-awareness. Richard Finneran's facing-page transcription of manuscripts for Tower (1928) reminds us often used drafts to explore a variety of thematic possibilities and would change emphasis or even settle on arguments at odds with those expressed his drafts. Nonetheless, manuscripts of Leda reveal a thematic consistency with final form of poem, and publication of drafts and foul papers presents readers not so much with alternative texts but rather with an additional context in which to read published versions of poem. contexts offered by drafts suggest found himself fascinated by possibilities inherent in depictions of resistance in face of brutality. In manuscripts, he emphasizes resistance and struggle, devoting considerable energy to fleshing out both divinity of Zeus and humanity of Leda. In published versions of poem, he explores potential of conflict and struggle to offer spiritual fulfillment, and in its final form poem takes on a grim and ironic tone regarding suffering and transcendence, even as it supports dignity of resistance. They stress dichotomy between divine and classical and create a parallel between Zeus's divinity and Leda.
