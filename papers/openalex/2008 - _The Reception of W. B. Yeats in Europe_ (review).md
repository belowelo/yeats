## Abstract

Reviewed by: The Reception of W. B. Yeats in Europe Russell McDonald The Reception of W. B. Yeats in Europe. Edited by Klaus Peter Jochum. London: Continuum, 2006. xxxvi + 359 pp. $250.00. Thirty-five years after his formative experience collecting folklore in the west of Ireland with Lady Gregory, Yeats recalled in Autobiographies (New York: Scribner, 1999), "when we passed the door of some peasant's cottage, we passed out of Europe as that word is understood" (299). Yeats's preference for the dreamy Celtic otherworld that those cottages represented for him over the cosmopolitanism of modern Europe is well known. French Symbolism certainly influenced him in the 1890s, and he read Dante, Balzac, and Nietzsche with great interest, but his favorite European writers tended to reaffirm his own preexisting views rather than to help him shape new ones. This valuable addition to the Athlone Critical Traditions Series: The Reception of British and Irish Authors in Europe reveals more clearly than ever how continental Europe has reciprocated Yeats's ambivalence toward it. "Certainly, Yeats has not become a popular or widely known poet in European countries," writes Klaus Peter Jochum in his introduction (4). The reasons for this are laid out in the book's fourteen chapters by an international group of Yeatsians, each of whom: provides an overview of Yeats's reception in a particular country or linguistic region; lists and evaluates significant translations of his works; summarizes important monographs and articles about him; and, in some cases, alludes to his influence on other poets. One important conclusion Jochum draws is that "Yeats himself was only moderately interested in ensuring a European reputation" (10). Though we hear repeatedly that his poems are hard to translate, he cared little about working with his translators. As a result, his plays were often translated before his poems, at least in Western Europe. Not until after he won the Nobel Prize in 1923 were any major efforts made to translate his poems in Germany, and in Italy such efforts waited until the 1930s. When his poems were translated earlier than that by Roland Holst in the Netherlands and Henri-D. Davray in France, his translators chose to present him almost exclusively in his Celtic Twilight phase, making him seem remote and inaccessible to most non-Irish readers. Interest in Yeats has been even less fervent in Eastern Europe. As Roger Keys observes of the reception in the former USSR, "The harvest of Yeats translations remained extremely meagre throughout almost the entire Soviet period . . . until the advent of . . . glasnost and cultural pluralism led to [End Page 415] stirrings of wider interest in the late 1980s" (144). One of Yeats's few works that actually deals with Eastern European subject matter, the early uncollected poem "How Ferencz Renyi Kept Silent" (first published in 1887), draws a parallel between Hungary and Ireland as "the Hungary of the West," yet no Hungarian translation appeared until 1971. The chapters on Yeats's reception in Hungary, in Croatia and Serbia, and in Romania are among the shortest in the book for the simple reason that they have scant material to cover, though only Romania has not had a significant increase of enthusiasm for Yeats since the fall of communism. As if to make up for this limited history in continental Europe, the volume devotes a disproportionate amount of space to Yeats's reception in Ireland: three chapters, totaling about 65 pages. Most of the information will be familiar to Yeatsians, though it's helpful to be reminded of the disdain he often met with at home, and some of the quotations from original articles and reviews are amusing. Shortly after Yeats's death in 1939, Aodh de Blacam wrote in the Irish Monthly, "A few exquisite lyrics, a handful of very fine poetic plays, a few delicately lovely essays, the whole making a volume of, perhaps, 400 pages—this is the sum total of the work of Yeats that will live: hardly more than a minor poet's permanent achievement" (225). More recently, much scholarly ink has been spilled over Yeats's engagement with fascism, and anyone seeking a concise overview of this debate could hardly do better...
