## Abstract

This final chapter uniquely weds Yeats's fascination for Eastern Christianity (as manifested in the early Christian Byzantium or Ireland) and his renewed interest in the Indian East (as inspired by his collaboration with "an Indian monk" from Maharashtra Shri Purohit Swami in the 1930s), viewing them as symptomatic of the same search for an "authentic" embodied spirituality that does not make sharp distinctions between the natural and the supernatural, the profane and the sacred, the physical and the metaphysical. The chapter closely reads two versions of A Vision , two Byzantium poems, and the abstruse "Supernatural Songs". The discussion begins by showing how the dialectics of the primary and the antithetical, the noumenal and the phenomenal in Yeats's mystical thinking are informed by his complex engagement with modern scientific and philosophical thoughts of Charles Darwin, T. H. Huxley, John Tyndall, Isaac Newton, Immanuel Kant, and others. The chapter then examines Yeats's high or late modernist poems "Sailing to Byzantium" and "Byzantium" to demonstrate how they are deeply invested in mystical questions and explorations of an embodied, antithetical nature. The discussion then extends into a reading of "Supernatural Songs", a sequence of lyrics which draws upon orthodox and heterodox mystical ideas and practices of the different Easts noted earlier. Read as later developments of Yeats's search for alternative forms of spirituality dating back to his early career discussed in Chapter 2, the Byzantium poems and "Supernatural Songs" as well as his late Indophilia yield new insights and nuances.
