## Abstract

W. B. Yeats, while an innovative thinker and consummate artist, was also a product of his time, an age that moved from the High Victorian era to the brink of World War Two.As The Edinburgh Companion to W. B. Yeats and the Arts makes clear, Yeats was an intellectual who sought to make sense of the world he lived in and the theories and philosophies that it produced, and particularly to advocate for the central role of the arts in modern life.He absorbed the key ideas on artistic production, aesthetics, and the function of the arts in contemporary society and transformed them in line with his own agendas and aims.Formative to his understanding of the character of art and craft was his reading of the works of John Ruskin and William Morris, the latter of whom he knew personally, and which prompted him to seek the restoration of what was widely perceived as the lost unity in the arts created by the negative impact of modernity.Rather than advocating Morris's radical socialist views, Yeats presented the reformer, as Lucy Hartley and John Whittier-Ferguson argue, as 'a pre-modern dreamer', a conservative in the word's sense of 'protection', 'defence' and 'preservation' (p.19).Yeats's artistic development, they contend, should be understood as a story of resistance to Morris's social and aesthetic practices.Colin Trodd, in his essay on Yeats, Blake, and the Romanticism of the Arts and Crafts Movement, similarly notes that the poet constructed Morris as 'a romantic subject whose pantheistic language of sensuous intuition connects him to a community of imagination committed to the view that God's immanence in the world process is the subject of art' (p.143).In material terms the contrast of the pared-down design of the Dun Emer Press can be seen as evidence of Yeats's advocation of 'simplification through intensity' (p.27) in contrast to the elaborate graphic design of Morris's Kelmscott Press.The productions of Dun Emer, and subsequently Cuala Press, were collaborative ventures, however.W. B.'s determination to dominate his sisters' enterprise is evident in his masterminding of the 1935 and 1937 editions of A Broadside, as outlined here by Angela Griffith.Ultimately the materiality of these printed sheets, a fusion of traditional balladry, contemporary writing, imagery, and orality reflect the plurality of the new Irish state in 1930s, while also providing the reader with a more universal site of sensory exploration.Beyond Ruskin and Morris, Yeats's understanding of the potential of the role of the arts was affected by the Symbolist movement, Richard Wagner's ideas on the Gesamtkunstwerk, and Friedrich Nietzsche's incitement to create a dynamic combination of the arts.
