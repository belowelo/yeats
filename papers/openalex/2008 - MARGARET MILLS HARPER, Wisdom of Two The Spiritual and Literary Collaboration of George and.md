## Abstract

THE part played in the creation of W. B. Yeats's work by his wife George was underestimated for many years. She tended to be eclipsed on the romantic side by Maud Gonne and on the literary side by Lady Gregory. Moreover, while her role as the medium of the spiritual knowledge underpinning A Vision (1925 and 1937) was indisputable, the literary significance of this collaborative process and of the philosophical system it produced were downplayed. Recently this situation has changed, as new work has sought to re-evaluate the nature and significance of George's role, most notably in Ann Saddlemyer's brilliant biography Becoming George: The Life of Mrs W. B. Yeats (2002). Margaret Mills Harper's ambitious monograph, Wisdom of Two: The Spiritual and Literary Collaboration of George and W. B. Yeats, builds on this biographical reassessment to explore critically the Yeats's marriage as an imaginative collaboration. This approach is particularly illuminating when it comes to A Vision, the work most closely related to their joint spiritual explorations. Harper helped to edit two volumes of Yeats's Vision Papers (1992–2001), a project that was overseen by her late father, George Mills Harper, himself a leading scholar of Yeats's interest in the occult. So unsurprisingly her study is informed by a deep familiarity with the thousands of pages of automatic script, dream notebooks, and drafts that lie behind the two, markedly different, published versions of the idiosyncratic philosophical system.
