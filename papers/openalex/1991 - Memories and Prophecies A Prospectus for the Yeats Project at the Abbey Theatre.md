## Abstract

Yeats's dramatic vision and accomplishment are increasingly recognised as among the most significant and exciting of the twentieth century. T. S. Eliot, of course, considered Yeats as both a pioneer and model in the effort to restore verse drama to the modern stage: "Yeats had nothing, and we have had Yeats …. We can begin to see now that even the imperfect early attempts he made are probably more permanent literature than the plays of Shaw."1 Eric Bentley, the dean of American drama critics, describes Yeats as "the only considerable verse dramatist in English for several hundred years".2 Rooted in a uniquely Irish heritage of myth, music, metaphysics and poetry, the theatre envisioned by Yeats traces its ancestry back to the ancient Greeks and invites comparison with the music drama of Wagner as well as the ceremonial art of the Japanese No. In attempting to express new forms of consciousness appropriate to the modern age, Yeats also foreshadowed the work of such revolutionary theatre artists of our own time as Peter Brook, Jerzy Grotowski and Robert Wilson.
