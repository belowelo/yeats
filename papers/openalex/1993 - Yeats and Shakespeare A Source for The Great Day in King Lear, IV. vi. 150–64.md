## Abstract

Here, indeed, is a poem where Yeats's self-elected role of "the wild old wicked man" in his last poems comes off at its best. The poet's disillusionment and rage at the mayhem of political revolutions is so complete here that the rhetoric of "The Great Day" recalls the ravings of King Lear on the fields near Dover. As a matter of fact, Lear's famous tirade against "authority" and "justice" seems to have been at the back of Yeats's mind while composing his short poem. I cite below King Lear, IV. vi. 150–64, for comparison: Lear. What! art mad? A man may see how this world goes with no eyes. Look with thine ears: see how yond justice rails upon yond simple thief. Hark, in thine ear: change places, and, handy-dandy, which is the justice, which is the thief? Thou hast seen a farmer's dog bark at a beggar? Glou. Ay, sir. Lear. And the creature run from the cur? There thou might'st behold The great image of Authority: A dog's obeyed in office. Thou rascal beadle, hold thy bloody hand! Why dost thou lash that whore?
