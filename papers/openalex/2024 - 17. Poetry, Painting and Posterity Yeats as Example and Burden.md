## Abstract

This chapter looks at how later poets have negotiated with the precedent of Yeats's ekphrastic verse. It outlines how portraiture operates as something of a master trope in Yeats's poetics, and also notes a perceived homology in play between portraiture and the poetic genre of lyric. This forms the basis for a series of close readings of poems by Medbh McGuckian, Sinéad Morrissey and Seamus Heaney. Yeats's complex, overlapping investments in portraiture and the lyric, often invoked as intertexts, offer a clear starting point for these poets' contrasting engagements with vision and the visual arts. McGuckian and Morrisey's poems are shown to engage critically with the manner in which women were viewed and represented both by Yeats and more generally within the Irish imagination. Heaney's preceding confrontation is more invested in the complex dynamics of artistic self-making. Nevertheless, the chapter identifies common and distinctly Yeatsian ground in how all these poets affirm the aesthetic and cognitive benefits of straddling the borders between the visual and the verbal arts.
