## Abstract

Known for its desire to make it new, the early twentieth century saw important shifts in poetic forms and in poetry's relationship to wider culture. During the same time, museums' sense of the culture they displayed and the public they served shifted as curators tried to teach wider audiences how to understand culture. Such modernist poets as W. B. Yeats, Ezra Pound, and Marianne Moore found in museums ways of negotiating culture and addressing readers. By reinflecting modernism with underdiscussed elements of the material record and by reading these writers' work for evidence of exhibitionary practice, this study shows how modernist poetry relied on museums for literary strategies, educational methods, and wider intellectual communities. Chapter One builds a context of early twentieth-century curatorial writings for the study's examination of modern poetry. Reading two of William Carlos Williams' poems in this light creates a model for discussing poems written directly about museum experiences and poems which draw more broadly on museum culture. Chapter Two shows how Yeats used visits to the Municipal Gallery of Modern Art (Dublin) to negotiate a path between aesthetics and Irish nationalism. Linking poetic exhibits of memory with this national art gallery, Yeats gave private history national importance. Chapter Three studies Pound's long involvement with the British Museum and Library (London) to consider the role of that museum's collection, display methods, and intellectual community in Pound's literary projects. In this institution Pound made himself a modernist, and strains of his experience there run through writings spanning his career. Chapter Four pairs Moore with the American Museum of Natural History (New York) to see Moore's own interest in collecting and early twentieth-century curatorial debates in relation to her adaptation of the AMNH's famous habitat dioramas in poems. Considering her material shaping of her books shows how Moore molded readers' experience with poems on the page. Chapter Five considers late twentieth-century readers' material and narrative constructions of the historical museum called modernism. This interdisciplinary study illuminates an insufficiently considered period in museum history, thereby revaluating modernist poetry, its aesthetic and political demands, and its material production.
