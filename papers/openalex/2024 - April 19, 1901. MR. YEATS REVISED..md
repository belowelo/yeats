## Abstract

We hope, in one sense, that Mr. Fisher Unwin will always be producing new editions of Mr. Yeats's poems; for Mr. Yeats 1 is one of the few remarkable poets who are really remarkable men, men who would have been as great in private as in public life. Even when he was a quite obscure poet, no one ever thought of calling him a minor poet. But in another sense we hope that there will be no more editions, for we prefer the original text of the poems, and Mr. Yeats is eternally altering it. He has probably learnt this from Mr. George Moore, whose books are revised with as much regularity as the British Constitution, or Whitaker's Almanack. The process may be suitable enough in the case of realistic novels, which stand or fall by the reality of their details; but it is simply ruinous in the case of poems, which are the produce of particular moods, no more to be reproduced than sunsets. Mr. Yeats has simply no right to alter a poem; it was not he that wrote it, but another man, the man of a moment, who will never live again. The moment a poem has really passed out of him, it no more belongs to him than St. Paul's Cathedral belongs to him. It is the heritage of the universal soul, like the works of Plato or Shakespeare. This truth is sufficiently clearly shown by the mere fact that Mr. Yeats, when he attempts to alter his poems, cannot alter them intelligently. The fact is, he has forgotten what they meant. Such poems as 'The Land of Heart's Desire' 2 and 'The Man who Dreamed of Fairyland' are probably the only poems in the world that he can never read or appreciate. In order to sustain our view of the destructive character of the poet's improvements, it will be better, perhaps, to confine ourselves to one typical poem, rather than to attempt to sum up the character of all. Mr. Yeats wrote a fine poem, as plain and clear as a flash of lightning, called 'A Cradle Song'. It began: The angels are bending Above your white bed, They weary of tending The souls of the dead.
