The stated aim of this book is to remedy a serious deficiency in scholarly writing about Yeats: namely, that 'there ha[s] been little thought given to the formal pro perties of the poems' (p. xiii). Helen Vendler's book certainly does give thought to these properties in a large number of gratifyingly sensitive and occasionally original close readings. Yet in order to gain an accurate sense ofwhat itaccomplishes, it is necessary to be clear about a range of things to do with formal properties which it does not attempt. This isnot, for instance, thekind of technical treatise inwhich the proportion of prosodic analysis to thematic interpretation is constantly high. Nor is iteven of the kind inwhich there is a continual toing and froing between tech nical analysis and thematic questions. On the contrary, readings tend to follow the trajectory of broad thematic lines,which are sporadically, though usually decisively, modified by discussion of form: of stylisticqualities, or the role of formal devices, or the handling of the chosen lyric form. Indeed, what may constitute a 'formal property' is conceived quite widely. This is despite the fact that the book, on a superficial glance, appears tobe largely concerned with different types of lyric form and their different stanza shapes and metres. The chapters are devoted to various kinds, with one devoted to sonnets, another to ottava rima poems, and so on. Nor, of course, is the book's actual breadth of understanding of form tobe criticized. However, such a broad conception does call intoquestion thebook's central claim that it is supplying a deficiency.
