Ms. Vendler's Poets Thinking was inspired, she tells us in the essay on Pope that forms one of the four parts of this book, by the need to defend that eighteenth-century writer from a 1983 English Institute panel of modern ''thinkers''—a philosopher, a political scientist, and an anthropologist—who undertook to evaluate Pope's Essay on Man from their perspectives. ''The panelists ended, with a certain complacency, by concluding that we were wiser than Pope, and that [such] poems . . . must be relegated, as outdated artifacts, to the museum of cultural attitudes.'' Ms. Vendler argues, on the contrary, that when persons from other disciplines, or even our own, approach poems, too often they do not know how to examine how poets think. What they examine is rather a translation of the poem into its ''conceptual paraphrase.'' Examining the same poem as the English Institute panel, Ms. Vendler shows not only how looking at a poet thinking differs from looking at a paraphrase of what he thought, but also reveals the ''peculiar stresses'' to which ''ideas'' are subject ''when they are incorporated into powerful poetry.'' She has written excitingly on a wide variety of poetry, but this is possibly her only—certainly her most extensive— foray into the eighteenth century. It should not be missed. The chapter on Pope—or rather, on passages from the Essay on Man—depends on the notion that the very brevity of poetry implicitly parodies the didactic genres it adopts, when it adopts one. She points out, for example, that when Pope asks a question that might come from a sermon (''Presumptuous man! . . . Why [wert thou] formed so weak . . .'') his answer is not ''to teach you humility'' but a ''preposterous mirror-image question'': (''Why formed no weaker, blinder, and no less''). As we experience the shock of near farce, we are forced to feel the folly of the whining complaints man addresses to his creator. Likewise, she continues, when Pope plays logician (''For this plain reason: Man is not a fly''), he bases his argument not on laborious demonstration, but rather on ''enact[ing] the experience of what it would be like to possess overdeveloped senses.'' On the general issue of ''ideas'' in poetry, she concludes persuasively that in poetry, ideas are ''framed, posed, choreographed, heightened, and refined or coarsened . . . recommended as wonderful artifacts of human mentality, apt for exhibition, rather than as guides for life or as philosophical axioms.'' Of course we can object that there is no reason why they cannot be recommended both as wonderful artifacts and as guides or axioms. Her perspective is 184 rewarding, though her account of the poem is all too brief and focused on very familiar passages. Yet the best part of the book, for the lover of eighteenth-century poetry, may not be the chapter on Pope at all. Rather, it lies in her introduction, for eighteenth-century poetry that is not satiric has generally been very little—and rather poorly—read since its own time. Who now reads Young's Night Thoughts? Or Cowper, other than ''The Castaway''? Or even the nonfeminist poems of the women writers? When I first encountered Ms. Vendler's criticism, many years ago, I realized that her approach enabled me to appreciate such writers in a way that strategies appropriate for metaphysical poems or Romantic poems or eighteenth-century satires did not, though her methods also work for such poems.
