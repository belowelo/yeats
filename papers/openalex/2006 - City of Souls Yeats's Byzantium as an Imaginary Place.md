## Abstract

There are eight million stories in naked city but no master narrative. Real cities are chaotic if not quite random, noisy and confused, immersed in what Dickens in Little Dorritt calls the usual uproar. Imaginary cities, however, are not bound by vagaries either of history or daily life and can take on any qualities that their creator chooses to give them. Since they come from one mind and express one sensibility, such places generally have an underlying principle that defines their nature, whether or not this is creator's intention. In case of Yeats, haunted all his life by mythology of pagan Ireland, managed improbable feat of transforming an imperial capital on Bosphorus into a version of Celtic Otherworld. To anyone but Yeats this conflation might seem strange, but it was his core belief that: Many times man lives and dies Between his two eternities That of race and that of soul And ancient Ireland knew it all. (Poems 325) In particular, ancient Ireland knew that world of senses is coterminous with another, invisible world that is equally real but generally inaccessible to mortal men and women. This is just as well for most part, since inhabitants of Otherworld, sidhe, are dangerous simply through intensity of their presence and even a glimpse of one of them, if not fatal immediately, can lead to a lifetime of wandering and obsession as in Stories of Red Hanrahan and Song of Wandering Aengus. Sometimes domain of sidhe is all around us, sometimes it is located in faery mounds throughout Ireland, and sometimes it is an island or archipelago far to West, in ocean. It can be reached in a moment of transcendent insight, but more often through death, which is why it is so often visualized as underground or in direction of setting sun. The dancing spirits of Byzantium reach this strange shore in same way. The sidhe of ancient stories are not small and not cute; they are warriors, as they are in poems like Hosting of Sidhe. Yeats, however, added another attribute to their existence by making them dancers as well, even in earliest of his poems such as Stolen Child: Where wave of moonlight glosses The dim grey sands with light, Far off by furthest Rosses We foot it all night, Weaving olden dances, Mingling hands and mingling glances Till moon has taken flight. (Poems 18) It was, for Yeats, a short step from having sidhe dance to having dead dance as well. He knew that pagan Irish believed in metempsychosis, and that sidhe were bound to earth both in mythology and, later, in Catholic teaching; it was natural to make human ghosts on their way to their next life take on qualities of immortal spirits. Yeats would never have said that ghosts are beings of Irish mythology and folklore, since they are a different race, but throughout his work he allowed these classes of spirits to converge. By time he wrote (or transcribed) A Vision, even Irish peasants, connoisseurs in this matter, cannot tell difference: When moon's full those creatures of full Are met on waste hills by country men Who shudder and hurry by.... (62) There is considerable irony here, since theme of early poems is complete separation of human existence and sidhes' existence--the stolen child, as that poem goes on to say, will never return to human life, and wandering Aengus will never emerge from hollow lands and hilly lands. But in re-visioning of A Vision human spirits purged of mire and blood exist in same manner as sidhe except that they are not freed from cycle of eternal return. The blood-begotten spirits of Byzantium, then, are a natural outgrowth both of Yeats's immersion in pagan mythology and his belief that many times man lives and dies. …
