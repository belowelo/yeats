## Abstract

On 2 April 1916, the first performance of W. B. Yeats's famous play At the Hawk's Well took place. 1 Yeats described it as one "in which masks are being used for the first time in serious drama in the modern world" (L 610).The masks and costumes were designed by Edmund Dulac, who collaborated closely with Yeats on the play.Dulac also provided illustrations of them for its first print publication, in the March 1917 issue of Harper's Bazar.It has been observed that some of these masks and costumes are strikingly Egyptian-looking.Dorsey Kleitz claims, for instance, that the "hawk was unabashedly Egyptian in origin," as was the "hawk's head decorating the black cloth ceremonially unfolded and folded at the start and conclusion of the play" (52).He believes, however, that these Egyptian features say "more about the London vogue for Orientalist exotica" than anything more profound about the play itself.This article will, instead, explore the possibility that the "Egyptian faces" of At the Hawk's Well are, after all, more deeply rooted in Yeats's vision for the play.It will do so by tracing the development of Yeats's engagement with Egyptian mythology and how it may have contributed to the genesis of At the Hawk's Well.When Yeats wrote on 10 April 1896 to a recent initiate of the Hermetic Order of the Golden Dawn, of which the Irish poet had been a member since 1890, he explained to him that "Egyptian faces [may] very well come to you after your initiation, as the order is greatly under Egyptian influence" (CL 2: 19).Indeed, its headquarters were the Isis-Urania
