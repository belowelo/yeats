But after this carrot has been dangled, Carnduff, the leading exemplar of such work by ex-servicemen, is deemed to be 'riddled with facile rhymes, studied archaisms, heightened chivalric idioms, and figurativecliches, and his themes seldom depart from sounding out the customary elegiac or jingoistic platitudes one finds in thousands of other glibly written war poems' (p. 199). In such a manner, the book becomes curiouslyself-dismissing. As with the historical and cultural context, Haughey's problem is his lack of a coherent argument. He constantlybemoans the tendency of Irishliterarycritics to interpretworks of art in terms of identity politics, asking us to bear in mind the legitimacy of more purely aesthetic criteria.But he himself patently lacks any such criteria, and perpetuallyfalls back on vexingly ill-defined notions of ethnicity and identity. Generally, he will immediately follow such lapses by reasserting the primacy of aesthetic taste, and ask us to consider his next batch of poets on their own terms;but he repeatedlyfails to offer any reason why we ought to do so. The book, therefore,is disappointingand a missed opportunity.Haughey triesto read bad poems as if they were good ones, and thus he does a disservice to his archive of ballads and laments, which, in other hands, will surelyyet be revealed as a rich treasuretrove of popular culture. More basically,he never once stops to consider,beyond and above the matterof Ireland,what might be at stakein talking about warpoetry.What iswar poetry?What shouldit do?What might we expect of it? A basic take on such primal, pertinacious, and irresolvable questions would surely be a prerequisitefor Haughey's undertaking;but it does not seem to have occurredto him to ask them.
