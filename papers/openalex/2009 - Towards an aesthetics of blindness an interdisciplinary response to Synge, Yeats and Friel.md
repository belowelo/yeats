## Abstract

David Feeney. Towards an Aesthetics of Blindness: An Interdisciplinary Response to Synge, Yeats, and Friel. New York: Peter Lang, 2007. isbn 978-0-8204- 8662-8 hbk 331pp £40.00 In his essay multiculturalism, Politics of Recognition, Charles Taylor discusses the question of how we might arrive at a just evaluation and understanding of different cultures. Drawing upon Hans-Georg Gadamer's notion of a fusion of horizons, his argument is that any serious engagement with difference necessitates the development of vocabularies of comparison (67). What this means is that the process by which we come to judge hitherto culturally alien forms must come about on the basis of an understanding of what constitutes worth that we couldn't possibly have had at the beginning. We have reached the judgment partly through transforming our standards (67). Something of this presumption underpins David Feeney's endeavour to articulate the conceptual, representational, and experiential contours of what he terms the aesthetics of blindness. Feeney's starting point is the ocularcentrism of a philosophical tradition in which visual experience is privileged as the primary site of aesthetic appreciation. His argument proceeds by asking how blindness-of the literal rather than metaphorical kind-disrupts both the logic of that ocularcentrism but also demands the development of a whole new way of conceptualising aesthetic experience. The central chapters of the book offer a sustained analysis of the relationship between blindness and aesthetic appreciation as explored in plays by Synge, Yeats, and Friel. This is followed by a discussion of a range of first-person narratives by Stephen Kuusisto, John Hull, and Georgina Kleege (among others), all of which engage with the question of the accessibility of visual beauty and the possibility of conceptualising a nonvisual aesthetic. At the centre of Feeney's inquiry is the notion of aesthetic experience as somehow ineffable and intrinsically untranslatable. The question to which the book returns again and again is whether blind people can actually appreciate forms of visual beauty: Does the restructuring of the hierarchy of the senses in blindness mean that blind people, having to make do with verbal descriptions of the arrangement and signifi- cance of sites of visual beauty, invariably appreciate something other than the beauty in question which can only be known experientially? Or can visual beauty be experienced directly through non-visual channels of perception? Can hand, ear, nose, body, memory, vocabulary, and imagination be applied to this perceptual task, or are they redundant in the presence of a beauty that manifests itself visually? (45) In the end, Feeney suggests that forms of visual beauty are simply untranslatable, or at least that which makes them beautiful, and what constitutes an aesthetic appreciation of that beauty, will always be unattainable by the blind. The implication here is that verbal description can never capture what it is to look upon and visually experience a work of art. Descriptive language produces another kind of object in which the immediacy and 'wholeness' of a particular aesthetic order is inevitably lost in translation. Citing the dialogue between the philosophers Bryan Magee and Martin Milligan, Feeney suggests that the latter's insistence that he could appreciate forms of visual beauty as mediated through the descriptive language of others is little more than a misapprehension of the distinction between aesthetic and discursive order. For Feeney, any process of translation or description involves the decomposition of aesthetic order. In other words, what Milligan imagines he is doing when he appreciates descriptions of visual beauty is simply reducing natural wholes, which lay beyond the reach of his interpretive faculties, into their individual components (241). From this, he might gain knowledge and understanding but he will not, Feeney suggests, appreciate the visual beauty of things. …
