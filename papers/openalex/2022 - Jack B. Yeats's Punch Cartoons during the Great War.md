## Abstract

Jack B. Yeats's Punch Cartoons during the Great War* Brian Griffin (bio) Art critics, biographers, and other writers interested in the work of Jack B. Yeats have tended to concentrate on his "high" art, particularly his paintings dating to the first half of the twentieth century.1 In contrast to the close scrutiny that Yeats's paintings have received in the scholarly literature, his copious comic-strip and cartoon output in British comics and satirical magazines has received relatively slight attention, even though he created this material throughout most of his working life and such works outnumbered by far the paintings that he produced.2 As Michael Connerty explains in his article on Yeats's comic strips in various British comics in the 1890s and early [End Page 254] 1900s, Yeats's career "has hitherto almost exclusively been evaluated from the perspective of art history and with a focus on his paintings, meaning that his popular, ephemeral output has been pushed out to the periphery." In Ireland "there has been a tendency to articulate Yeats in terms of national identity and thus avoid recognition of his engagement with and contribution to British popular culture," such as his cartoon and comic-strip work. Connerty argues that scholars, critics, art galleries, and other institutions "have contributed to the processes of hierarchization and canonization" around Yeats's work that have obscured the artist's substantial corpus of comic-strip material.3 He has made a significant contribution toward positioning Yeats's comic strips in Victorian and Edwardian British comics into his overall oeuvre,4 but Yeats's other main cartoon material, his drawings in Punch magazine, remains relatively neglected by scholars. Apart from a single cartoon published on 9 May 1896,5 Yeats's contributions to Punch date from 2 November 1910. He continued to publish his drawings in the magazine on a regular basis until February 1941, with the exception of a final cartoon that made its appearance after a lengthy hiatus in the edition of 1 December 1948. All of these cartoons bore the pseudonym of "W. Bird" or "WBird" rather than Yeats's actual name.6 Yeats scholars have speculated as to why he used a pseudonym for his Punch drawings. Connerty suggests that he posed as "W. Bird" for fear that his image as an artist might have been damaged should it become public knowledge that he was the creator of such apparently frivolous material. On a slightly different but related note, Fintan O'Toole argues that Yeats resorted to using a pseudonym to sustain the illusion that as a modernist artist he was above such mundane matters as needing to work for a [End Page 255] living. As O'Toole states, "The artist had to be seen as a member of a species different from, and even hostile to, the jobbing craftsman." O'Toole furthermore suggests that Yeats, a nationalist, did not want to be openly associated with a publication that, as John Booth states, "epitomised John Bull at his most jingoistic."7 It is also possible that Yeats may have been attempting to hide his involvement with Punch from his older brother William Butler Yeats, who had been ridiculed in the magazine on a number of occasions before Yeats began contributing cartoons to it and remained a figure of fun in its pages for several years afterward.8 Yeats's Punch material has received sketchy acknowledgment or assessment from scholars.9 According to Hilary Pyle, the Punch cartoons are of interest mainly for their tentative link to Yeats's development as a painter; in her opinion, "if this comic work had little to do with the overall pattern of his painting in this period other than illustrating a more mature style of draughtsmanship, the feeling for subject-matter, where a brief caption added meat to a scribble, at least was kept alive and reminded him of the importance of the titles to his paintings."10 She asserts that Yeats drew the cartoons presumably because "he needed an assured income while he was establishing himself as an oil painter," but also because he "needed an outlet [End Page 256] for his natural frivolity."11 In a...
