## Abstract

In The Poetics of Utopia, Stewart Cole takes on the task of writing a monograph entirely dedicated to understanding poetry as a form of utopian literature. Cole argues that the intricate relationship between form and content, and indeed, the tensions extending from this relationship, render poetry ideal for expressing nuanced visions of utopia. His decision to use the work of W. B. Yeats (1865-1939) and W. H. Auden (1907-73) as his case studies is rooted in their politically conscious responses to two turbulent historical periods. Through analysis of the work of both poets, The Poetics of Utopia convincingly insists upon 'how loomingly persistent are our longings after better futures and how apt is poetry as a medium for refracting such longings' (p. 25). The Poetics of Utopia is broken into five main chapters. Each poet is afforded two chapters evaluating their individual work whilst the final chapter offers a cross comparison. Cole focuses on the more well-known and extensively analysed works of each poet to highlight the possibility of new readings made available by considering poetry as utopian literature. In the first chapter, Cole focuses on the nation-building evident in Yeats's early work, examining how he used Irish mythology in poems like 'The Song of Wandering Aengus' to inspire utopian desire. Chapter 2 traces the older Yeats's rejection of his role as the poet of the nation to his espousing of art's ability to grant visions of utopia which are otherwise impossible in reality - that is, the idea of art as utopia. Chapters 3 and 4 adhere to a similar structure in approaching Auden's poetry. The former covers the period up to the end of the 1930s and Auden's move to America, highlighting how his use of 'topographical motifs' (p. 28) and his search for 'personal unity' (p. 95) are utopian gestures. In Chapter 4, Cole argues that Auden's later poetry, by rejecting material possibilities of utopia and instead embracing Christian visions of the paradisial afterlife, emphasizes the gap between humanity's utopian desires and their future actualization. The final chapter effectively compares Yeats's 'Lapis Lazuli' and Auden's 'The Shield of Achilles', investigating the intensification of each poet's understanding of art's potential to impact their realities, be that at 'civilisational or celestial levels' (p. 190).
