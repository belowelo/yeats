## Abstract

The range of Jack B. Yeats's writing spans from the early plays for the miniature theater and broadside verses to the later idiosyncratic fiction and drama. The drama was performed infrequently, which would satisfy the wish of the narrator of Sligo who states: "If one performance only was given, that performance would have almost the force of an actual event." 1 Regarding prose, Jack Yeats's narrator claims that the very same book may be the last one written. On one level these statements seem slight, humorous, and ironic; on another they provide insight to a developing aesthetic. During the 1920s, as his painting drew critical recognition, he refused their reproduction except by art students. 2 The shift from illustration to more personal painting, from representation to abstraction, developed at the time of his mature writing and of the above thoughts from Sligo. Accompanying these changes are the concepts of immediacy, privacy, and memory that became the driving forces of both his writing and his picture-making. That his first mature plays came to the public in book form in Apparitions (1933), testifies to the value of written language, as well as performance. Their date coincides with Sailing, Sailing Swiftly (1933), the second significant work of fiction, and reinforces Yeats's concern with the narrative or story mode. The novel incorporates pictures throughout the text, along with songs and other popular entertainments. Yeats's desire to reach beyond the usual confines of prose fiction is also found in Sligo (1930), where he experimented with linked memories, presented in the first person and placed randomly throughout the text, as in conversational style. In his picture-making, the narrative effect also comes about when title and picture are meshed in what some commentators classify as "literary" painting.
