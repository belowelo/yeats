## Abstract

THE relationship between the work of W. B. Yeats and its contexts is complex. Not only was Yeats an energetic public figure and cultural entrepreneur, but his poetry also intertwines the historic and the private in its mythologizing and revising of the poetic self. Scholars of the poet and his work have long immersed themselves in this contextual richness. However, as Nicholas Grene suggests in the introduction to Yeats's Poetic Codes, a focus on biography and on the work of historicizing the oeuvre has sometimes been pursued at the expense of paying due attention to 'the work of the poems' (3). His study shifts the emphasis back to the operations of the texts through a concentration on 'the codes of practice developed to create the poems and situate them in relation to the poet, the reader, and their worlds' (3). The use of the word 'code' to describe these practices is perhaps misleading, in that it suggests the book might offer a set of hermeneutic keys to unlock hidden meanings in Yeats's work; thankfully, it does nothing of the sort. Rather, Grene categorizes some of Yeats's poetry's more denotative procedures—in contrast also to the formalist focus of Helen Vendler's recent Our Secret Discipline: Yeats and Lyric Form (2007)—treating in separate chapters Yeats's use of dates, place names, grammatical tense and mood, and the deictic terms 'this', 'that', 'here', and 'there', as well as his shifting deployment of various images of beasts and birds, of a range of voices, and of recurrent keywords, such 'dream' and the oppositional pair 'bitter/sweet'.
