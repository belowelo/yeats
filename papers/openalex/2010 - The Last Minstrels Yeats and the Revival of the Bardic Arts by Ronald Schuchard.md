Crucially, this 'music of poetry' is rendered with respect to its own tune and textures, unlike those imposed by musicians and 'mini-singers' whom Yeats excoriated not merely because he was tone-deaf (a technical description rather than a term of abuse, apparently), but because the gauze of sound inwhich themusician wraps thewords betrays them with its arrogant imposition. Following Yeats's interest in the theme through private drawing rooms and lec ture halls, Schuchard is also aware of those 'country houses at the end of drives' containing what Auden naughtily described as Yeats's 'parish of richwomen', here inparticular Dorothy Wellesley, whose splendid house at Penn-on-the-Rocks, with its rich paintings, splendid furniture, and fine library of leather-bound volumes became something of a heaven-haven, like Lady Gregory's equally handy one in Ireland. Yeats included references to her in his poetry, and put her poems in his anthology, The Oxford Book ofModern Verse (an odd commission for someone who thought of Louis MacNeice as 'an extreme radical', p. 364). But of names demanding citation themost prominent are those of Florence Farr and Arnold Dolmetsch, the former a somewhat erratic chanting-companion whose 'traditional' costumes and sing-alongs to the psaltery might be intuited as bordering on kitsch.
