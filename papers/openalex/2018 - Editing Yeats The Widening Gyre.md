## Abstract

IF THERE IS AN ARGUMENT against literary critics attempting to be editors, it must hinge on the concept of pro-gress. No literary critic worth their salt can wholeheartedly believe that criticism of literature really progresses as such, becoming steadily more accurate and correct as the decades pass; whereas the good editor of literary texts, it may seem, is both committed to progress and its beneficiary, since editing depends on bodies of knowledge, or vast and often outsourced reserves of information, which do in fact develop and gain momentum over time, and can be put to work by means of theoretically coherent and consistent principles of operation. A 'new' edition of a poet, that is, might be supposed meaningfully in advance of the 'old' edition; whereas this week's literary critic, though knowing more (or different) things, is not guaranteed to be in any way ahead of predecessors from dec-ades (or from centuries) before. No literary critic can do the job properly while encumbered by much in the way of a theory of how to perform literary criticism; no literary editor, on the other hand, can very well function without some body of rules, even if these are substantially self-imposed ones.
