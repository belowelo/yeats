## Abstract

If we could continuously relate the literary development of Yeats and Joyce over the course of 50 years from the publication of 'The Wanderings of Oisin' in 1888 to that of Finnegans Wake in 1939, we would have a thread, maybe, that would serve to guide us through the labyrinth of the modern period in English literature. Certainly there are inducements enough to attempt to trace a continuous development which promise more than a wild-goose chase. In this chapter I propose to glance briefly at some of the inducements — some points of contact between Joyce and Yeats which suggest that further investigation would be worth while. I have not tried to refine my observations to any degree, partly because I want to gallop over a large territory alighting here and there and partly because I want to avoid so far as possible the repetition of, or detailed reference to, matters that have already had a good airing. Richard Ellmann's accounts of the relationship of Joyce and Yeats in his biography of Joyce and his Eminent Domain are assumed and I have kept off some but not all the areas already well posted by Ellmann and others as, for instance, Joyce's admiration for and use of Yeats's The Tables of the Law', his attack on Yeats and others in 'The Holy Office', and so on.
