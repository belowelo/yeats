## Abstract

SOUND is a central aspect of the experience of reading poetry. The way a poem first strikes a reader's inner ear is often more captivating than its subject-matter; while the characteristics of a poet's voice may remain in the mind long after their verse's more concrete details have faded. To note this is not original, of course. But to go beyond such banal observations and write in real depth about how poetry actually sounds is rather difficult. With music (and even then really only with western classical music), there is an agreed way of notating different pitches and rhythms, and so a set of linguistic terms through which to discuss its sound. Music is also generally held to exist primarily when performed. However, poetry is altogether more slippery. It exists concretely on the page and is performed, according to no set criteria, silently to the self or when read aloud. Furthermore, the terms in which critics traditionally attempt to describe the sound of poetry always seem to be tending towards the figurative—the poem's 'sound', the poet's 'voice' and the reader's 'inner ear' all gesturing towards something they do not quite denote.
