## Abstract

In recent years increasing attention has been paid to the use of complimentary therapies within the health service. Art therapy has been used in Britain since the 1940s mainly in mentally handicapped, geriatric and oncology units. Unfortunately to date extensive literature research has confirmed that few people have used art therapy in the treatment of diabetes or have evaluated its effectiveness.1 Naumberg in 1958 said 'the process of art therapy is based on the recognition that man's most fundamental thoughts and feelings, derived from the unconscious, reach expression in images rather than words'.2 When art therapy is used as a communication tool, it becomes a way of expressing personal feelings and thoughts, and permits a release of emotion that might have otherwise been suppressed. As a diabetes specialist nurse I am fully aware of the emotional difficulties that patients have living with a chronic illness on a daily basis. I have trained in transactional analysis counselling; however on occasion I have found that counselling skills are not enough to facilitate behavioural strategies that allow patients to come to terms with their diagnosis. I am currently working with three patients using art therapy as a facilitation tool for communication and expression of feelings. This is one patient's case study. Mrs M is a 48 year old intelligent and articulate person who has suffered from insulin dependent diabetes for 22 years. This lady was referred to me by one of my medical colleagues at the end of her diabetes clinic appointment. It soon became clear that Mrs M was very distressed and upset at the way she had been treated during her consultation. 'It's like a conveyer belt in here' she said, 'you are not treated like a person only a disorder'. Mrs M agreed to share the reasons for her unhappiness with me. After several consultations I had the feeling that Mrs M had difficulty in truly expressing the way she felt towards her diabetes and the treatment she felt she had received from the medical profession since her initial diagnosis. Being aware that this lady listed art as one of her interests, I suggested that she might like to try and draw or paint how she felt about her diabetes. The idea sounded appealing to Mrs M and she returned two weeks later with her first painting. The photograph in Appendix 1 is an accurate image of the first painting produced. The picture is entitled The Cuckoo Clock. The clock face represents a vicious circle trying to achieve good control without necessarily achieving the desired results. Being a clock watcher for life. The penalties for not watching the clock are severe hypoglycaemic attacks in spite of her best efforts. The spinning whirlpool in the background of the picture is a representation of the struggle to stay on top of diabetes, not allowing it to dominate your life. The jagged lines represent the discomfort of 'staying on top of it'. Mrs M feels they point accusingly at her. The words on the rim of the painting represent the attitude and the messages from various health care professionals over the years. The numerals are Mrs M's hospital number: 'I know it backwards and wear it like a tattoo' she said. They reinforce the feeling of being less than human, the loss of identity and the feeling of being just another case on the clinic's conveyer belt. Mrs M goes on to say 'the whole unique person is deliberately ignored; I always come away feeling that I have never been seen at all'. The numerals also represent eating times, which may be appropriate to Mrs M but not to others in a social setting. The feeling of being different is reinforced and may lead to isolation and embarrassment. The heavy plunger is representative of the burden of having diabetes. The un-requested responsibility, the possible consequences of non-compliance, the daily decision making, all of which rob this patient of her spontaneity and impulsiveness: 'the delights' that would give her life spice. The bold marks on the syringe depict the importance of getting the insulin dose right each time. Getting it wrong makes all the difference to this patient's life in the next few hours and in the long term. The label Mrs M suggests 'is the most de-humanising part of being diabetic; it is given at the time of diagnosis by medics and is quickly adopted by your closest family and friends. Everyone seems to like it except me. I have enough to contend with, understand and try to accept at diagnosis without being given some alien label'. She goes onto say 'you are expected to keep it because it is convenient for others and you do not wish to be seen as anything other than accepting of your new condition in case someone quickly slaps another label on you'. The chain a manacle for life depriving this patient of the freedom that she had prior to diagnosis. The wire mesh is a very open weave significantly at the forefront of the picture. It represents being held back from the outside world, but at the same time the weave is big enough to attempt escape to normality. The pattern on the outer clock-face Mrs M had originally depicted these as coffin shaped; however she then became self-conscious and decided that was too harsh. They would have represented the consequences of diabetes complications, which she feels hang over her like the Sword of Damocles. The message from this is strong and powerful and is the only incentive this lady feels she has had to maintain good control. 'If all of this doesn't make me cuckoo then I do not know what will' declares Mrs M. Evaluation of the art therapy is assessed by the use of a recognised and professionally accepted well-being questionnaire at two month intervals. In addition to this the patients and I reflect on our work detailing and recording the interpretation of each painting in a journal. The journals are kept by the patients and can be consulted at any time. The following is an extract from Mrs M's journal. 'I regret that the advice I have had about my diabetes over the years has not had a holistic emphasis. I feel that the picture I would have produced would have been happier and more optimistic. This is the first time in over twenty years that I have ever been allowed and encouraged to be perfectly honest about how I feel about my condition. Painting the picture helped me feel more whole. It was very liberating making me feel freer than I had in a long time. I wasn't embarrassing or upsetting anyone else by painting it'. It is widely acknowledged that the ability to communicate is an essential human characteristic. Art therapy gives some people an alternative language to examine and re-order a confused view of life. It can bring to awareness the significance of their present actions and feelings without the negotiation required in verbally mediated therapy.3 Naumberg reminds us that every individual, whether trained or untrained in art, has the latent capacity to project his inner conflicts into visual form. As I have only worked with a small number of patients to date it would be improper to generalise on findings at this stage. However in the case of Mrs M art therapy has undoubtedly been a valuable agent for therapeutic change. 1 Reproduced with kind permission of Medical Illustration, Gartnaval General.
