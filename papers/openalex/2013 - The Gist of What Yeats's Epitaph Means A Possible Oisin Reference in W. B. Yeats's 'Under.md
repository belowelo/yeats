## Abstract

ALTHOUGH prominent Yeats scholars such as R. F. Foster, James Pethica, Jon Stallworthy, and Helen Vendler have explained important aspects of references in W. B. Yeats's famous epitaph 'Cast a cold eye | On life, on death. | Horseman, pass by!'1 (which appears both on Yeats's gravestone and as the final three lines of Yeats's 'Under Ben Bulben'), one important possible reference has remained unremarked in scholarly treatments of the poem: the epitaph's possible reference to Oisin, the mythical Irish warrior-poet protagonist of Yeats's early narrative poem 'The Wanderings of Oisin'. Understanding the epitaph's possible reference to Oisin will help readers more fully understand the multi-layered references of 'Under Ben Bulben' and more fully get 'the gist of what they mean' (as the poem says when it first mentions 'superhuman' horsemen in its opening section).2 The horsemen of the poem's first section are apparently the mythical supernatural riders known as the Sidhe. Sam McCready explains Yeats' belief that these riders inhabited Ben Bulben and the landscape surrounding the mountain: 'The mountain had special associations for WBY. He climbed it as a boy and fished its streams, regarding it as a place inhabited by the Sidhe. At sunset, he believed, fairy riders swept down from the mountain over the plains of Drumcliff below.'3 McCready also explains that Yeats thought of Ben Bulben as a threshold to another dimension: 'WBY collected fairy lore around Drumcliff, and in his essay "Drumcliff and Rosses" he describes the door to fairyland, high on the slopes of Ben Bulben, from which the fairy riders emerge each evening to roam over the plain of Drumcliff below.'4
