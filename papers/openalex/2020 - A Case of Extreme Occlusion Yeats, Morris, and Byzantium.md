## Abstract

Yeats borrowed from William Morris, 'my chief of men', the conception of Byzantium as the location of a 'popular' art whose singular beauty reflected a culture characterized by the unity of opposites (geographical, social, religious, professional). This chimed with his own Blakean and Empedoclean model of reality as a dynamic system of interacting opposites, and his cultural programme for a disunited Ireland. Byzantium as conceived by Morris became a precondition for Yeats's cherished Unity of Being, and the symbol of his summum bonum. He incurred a debt to Morris so great that he could never bring himself to acknowledge it.
