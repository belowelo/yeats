## Abstract

'Works of art are always begotten by previous works of art', said Yeats.1 He expanded this idea by stressing repeatedly the importance of commitment to tradition and the impoverishment that divorce from tradition entails. Edmund Spenser, whose discontents in violent Ireland and changing England made him a kindred spirit, may have the best claim to being the principal 'begetter' of his early poems. Moreover, because of his early attachment to Spenser, Yeats's imagination became permanently immersed in what can legitimately be called the mutability tradition: that of reflection (elegiac, defiant, tragic, philosophical) on the subject of time and change. As well as Shakespeare in his sonnets, Keats, Swinburne, and Fitzgerald had been preoccupied with the subject of mutability; Wordsworth and Shelley had each written a sonnet so entitled; and, as has recently been demonstrated, it was a constant in the poetry of Yeats's older contemporary, Thomas Hardy, who presciently prefixed his first volume of poems with his own drawing of an hourglass. But it was Spenser – himself 'folowing' in this 'the ensample of Horace and Ouid'2 – whose ruminations on time and change fired Yeats's imagination. However, my purpose here is not so much to trace the influence of Spenser on Yeats as to use him as a precise starting point and a referential frame within which to map the ways in which the notion of destructive time and change invades Yeats's poetic engagement with almost everything in his personal, social, and political life.
