## Abstract

Abstract The fifth chapter traces the prominence of sculpture in Yeats's late poems. It begins with close readings of poems about Maud Gonne and Iseult Gonne, where statues or busts are invoked to address concerns with aging, loneliness, and a thwarted or unreciprocated desire. A comparative study of these sculptural love poems with the poems of Eva Gore-Booth and H.D. underscore the gender dynamics of Yeats's use of statues in verse. Subsequent sections connect Yeats's esoteric ideas about ancient statues in A Vision and a Rapallo Notebook to New Poems and Last Poems, where statues are nonstatic, seemingly organic, and consequently subject to aging or material degrading. The chapter concludes that Yeats's late "sculptural poetics" brings him into dialogue with Pound and T.S. Eliot. In his last poems he returns to and reanimates his early fantasies-as well as the material realities-of the art of sculpture.
