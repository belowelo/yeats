In one of those binary oppositions which we seem fated to employ whenever we describe our culture to ourselves, Yeats is opposed to Joyce, and the latter emerges as the preferred patron of modern Ireland. Where Yeats is atavistic, rural, romantic, mystical and committed to the heroic gesture, Joyce is modern, cosmopolitan, anti-mythological and unheroic. If the Irish are to take their predestined place on the European stage, it can only be, the argument goes, under the aegis of Joyce rather than Yeats. But is it really either modern or enlightened As we have argued elsewhere, the tragi-comic aspect of Irish intellectual life is to use a Joycean expression its belatedness.' In this article we wish to quarrel with the recent ideological reading of Yeats as advanced by Irish scholars. First of all, this reading involves a preoccupation with Yeats as a mythologizer, if not a mythomaniac. That he is the last great romantic no one disputes. But the crux of the matter for the Irish is that he 'invented' their Ireland in a way many now find unac300table. Dennis Donoghue tells us that there is a direct relation between 'Yeats's calling upon the hidden Ireland to come forth, by myth and personification, and his effort to define himself as a poet. The same imaginative idiom applies to both. Yeats's Ireland is a fiction; so is his Poet'.2 Seamus Deane has argued eloquently the case for abandoning the literary myths of the Revival and concludes that the 'glamorization of the Celt and of the Ascendancy was an attempt to reconcile on the level of myth what could not be reconciled on the level of politics' .3
