## Abstract

Why is it that Yeats's long poems remain, despite their acknowledged greatness, unexplored by criticism? Of the three most important of his long poemsNineteen Hundred and Nineteen , The Tower , and Meditation in Time of Civil Warnone has received more than a single examination of any length, and the last has never been discussed at all in the open forum of the quarterlies. The question rests finally on a more essential one: why do these poems, when their moving power convinces us of their significance, defy our attempts to make an approximate statement of their significance? I think the answer to both questions lies in the form of the long poem as Yeats created it, and specifically, in its structure. In his long poems Yeats fuses two structural modes, the discursive and the imagistic (or the direct and the oblique, to use Tillyarďs more familiar terms), which we are accustomed to find separate. The discursive mode in poetry depends upon the structural principle of syntax, a deploying of thought in logical, rational structures of statement; the imagistic mode depends upon the structural principle of the image, which replaces logical syntax with what Hart Crane called the logic of metaphor. One can usually speak of a poem's tendency in one direction or another, the discursive and the abstract or the imagistic and sensuous, for there are relative degrees of commitment to these principles, determined by the individual poet's own temperament and material. But by their very nature, poems require a solidity which the image can give, and thus it is rarely typical for a good poet to compose consistently in a discursive, relatively imageless mode, just as, before the twentieth century, it was rarely typical for a poet to write so totally dominated by the image as to become oblique. When we turn to Yeats's long poems, the special problem posed by them is that each one, by virtue of being a poem made up of poems, interrelated units in a series, can alternate these two dissimilar modes, letting a direct poem succeed an oblique one in the series.
