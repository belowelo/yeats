## Abstract

THE LIFE OF JACK YEATS BRUCE ARNOLD we see Jack B. Yeats through the rose-tinted glass of posterity, in which his place is secure, and imagine him always to have been loved and admired. We think he enjoyed the fame that now pours down upon his pictures in a golden shower, which is really a shower of gold, representing wealth and cupidity. He is judged to be great because he is wanted, and not because of any serious critical assessment that has been given to his work. For a painter regarded by Irish people, and by the world art markets, as having greatness in some measure or other, there is remarkably little critical analysis . And what there is comes largely from those whose admiration is a block to fair judgment. Everyone would love to have a Jack Yeats. The rich buy him for gilt-edged stock. The wise sell him to buy houses for their children . And the world looks on in wonder at this artist who occupies so special a place in Irish hearts. He expresses the life of the nation in an entirely unique way. He goes to the heart of it because he captures the personality and the actions of its people. The works are drawn from history, from the peasantry, from Irish myths and their heroes. They are taken from men who love horses and from horses that love men. They are inspired by his own love for circus clowns, singers, pavement artists, trick-o'-the-loop men and three-cardtrick -men. Jack Yeats has painted the life of Ireland into a thousand canvases and more. And because of that he will live forever. But recognition that this was so, in his work and in his life, came late and slow. It is we who have transformed him into a success, posthumously, because he is one now. He was hardly that, through the greater part of his life. Well into his sixties he almost lost his way as a painter, virtually giving up for a period of time and devoting his energies to novels and plays. In his seventies came recognition, but not the sale of his works. Into his eighties, when he was really ready to cease working, he sought to move onto a world stage, with a traveling exhibition in the United States, and THE LIFE OF JACK YEATS 302 his first solo show in Paris. Though not exactly rebuffed, these two events proved disappointing; and at the end of his life, contrary to what even the late events had foreshadowed, he seemed once again dogged by the perversities of fortune. Thus he recorded no great success abroad and, at the end, was confined to a narrow following in Ireland. All of his life he was admired by a small, trusting group of close friends. But world recognition in any wider sense came posthumously. Disentangling this story of Jack B. Yeats is by no means finished. Writing his biography1 has for me been a remarkable personal journey into the heart and soul of a country and of a man. What I discovered there confounds , perhaps more in an interpretative than in a factual sense, most of what we think we know about his life and about what he experienced. It seeks to reassess his emotional and artistic sources, his relationships, his state of mind, his artistic and intellectual development, the full nature of his nationalism. And in the process it casts aside many of the preconceptions that have been bred by a society determined to make him an icon of Irish nationalism. It also seeks to show that he consistently failed to "make the grade" in any worldly sense. He was never really very successful as an artist. Even after recognition following the National Gallery show in London, with William Nicholson, and the 1945 exhibition in Dublin, Jack Yeats suffered setbacks, which are referred to earlier. We can take easily enough a revised view of his supposed "success." He had an iron will, and he painted what he painted because he believed in it.
