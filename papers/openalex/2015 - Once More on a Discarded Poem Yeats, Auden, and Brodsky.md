## Abstract

Once More on a "Discarded Poem":Yeats, Auden, and Brodsky Grigory Kruzhkov Admirers of the poetry of Joseph Brodsky might also be acquainted with his long essay on "September 1, 1939" by W. H. Auden, a revised version of one of his university lectures. The essay is a brilliant example of close reading by Brodsky, no less detailed and meticulous than his analyses of "Novogodnee" by Marina Tsvetaeva or "Orpheus and Eurydice" by Rilke. However, some of Brodsky's central points regarding Auden are debatable. Indeed, one is the main enigma of the poem—the question of why Auden never republished it after 1945 and why he did not include it in his Collected Poems. Brodsky explains that the reason for Auden's decision involves the concluding line of the stanza eight: "We must love each other or die."1 Brodsky writes, "According to several sources, he did so because he found the line presumptuous and untrue. Because, he said, we must die anyway. He tried to change it but all he could come up with was 'We must love each other and die,' which would be a platitude with misleading air of profundity."2 But was Auden right to reject a poem for only one dubious line? Brodsky's answer is both yes and no. Although the line was not logical enough, it had a clear meaning before World War II: we must love each other, otherwise we shall have to kill each other. Taking into account the historical situation of that time, this reading of the line statement appears quite justified. Brodsky provides an alternative way to read the line, too: "all he had was a voice, and this wasn't heard or heeded—what followed was exactly what he predicted: killing" (LTO 352). The poet decided to suppress the poem "because he felt he was responsible for failing to avert what had happened, since the whole point of his writing this poem was to influence public opinion" (LTO 354). Such a reading of the line can hardly can be taken seriously. How could Auden, who had proclaimed his poetical credo [End Page 130] half a year earlier in his famous line: "Poetry makes nothing happen," try to avert the war in Europe by means of a poem and, moreover, punish it for failing to fulfill this impossible task?3 It is to the first version that we need to look, if we are to understand Auden's difficulty with the line "We must love each other or die." Here, we have the word of Auden himself. In his foreword to B.C. Bloomfield's Bibliography of his works, Auden said: Rereading a poem of mine, 1st September, 1939, after it had been published, I came to the line "We must love one another or die" and said to myself: "That's a damned lie! We must die anyway." So, in the next edition, I altered it to "We must love one another and die." This didn't seem to do either, so I cut the stanza. Still no good. The whole poem, I realized, was infected with an incurable dishonesty—and must be scrapped.4 Auden's explanation differs essentially from the version that Brodsky suggests in his essay. The problem is not hidden in one line but in the poem as a whole. What was the dishonesty that so grated on Auden's ears? It might be that the problem here is the political phraseology, and the vulgar class approach, that enters the poem from the fourth stanza on: "imperialism's face and the international wrong," "the lie of the Authority, whose buildings grope the sky," as well as average conservative commuters and "helpless governors" making them play "their compulsory game" in the eighth stanza ending with a series of slogans: Who can release them now,Who can reach the dead,Who can speak for the dumb? In these lines, Auden sounds almost like Vladimir Mayakovsky, a Soviet propagandist poet with a thunderous voice whose aspiration was also to, in Auden's phrase from the seventh stanza, "undo the folded lie" of politicians and newsmakers.
