## Abstract

This chapter considers how the 1890s publications of Yeats's 'The Lake Isle of Innisfree' were instrumental in shaping its interpretation and Yeats's authorial identity. Scholars like George Bornstein argue that Yeats's revisions created a carefully constructed public image, with each book offering not just poems but a portrayal of the poet himself. Yeats's editing of his work reflects a desire to shape both his canon and public self. The chapter notes that in Yeats's 1895 Poems, his works embody a 'fictive Yeats,' an idealized Irish poet of tradition, legend, and beauty. This image is further curated and adjusted through his affiliations with publications like Henley's periodical National Observer and Sharp's anthology Lyra Celtica, which promoted Yeats's work as distinctly Irish and tied to Celtic heritage. Additionally, in anthologies such as The Treasury of Irish Poetry, Yeats's poetry was positioned as the culmination of Irish poetic tradition in English, yet devoid of overtly political dimensions. Thus, each publication context or envelope influenced how Yeats circulated in the public domain, emphasizing varied aspects from mysticism and Irishness to aesthetic purity and national identity.
