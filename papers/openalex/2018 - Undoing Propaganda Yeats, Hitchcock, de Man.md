## Abstract

Undoing Propaganda:Yeats, Hitchcock, de Man Christopher Morris (bio) Restricting the definition of propaganda has proven to be difficult.1 Its present sense of "political persuasion" is an extension of its origin in a papal bull issued in the Counter-Reformation by the Roman Catholic Church, establishing a committee of bishops to be known as the Congregation for the Propagation of the Faith, whose mission was proselytization.2 That religious provenance became secularized in modernity when the dissemination of doctrine was appropriated by nation-states.3 Since advocacy on behalf of non-state ideologies, like communism or jihadism, has also been considered propaganda, the application of the term has further broadened. In fact, the numerous perspectives on the subject offered by the recent Oxford Handbook of Propaganda Studies fail to distinguish propaganda conceptually from ideology, advertising, or any persuasive discourse, including fiction. In Literature and Propaganda, A.P. Foulkes commends literature's potential to demystify propaganda but concedes that both can function in the same way, and that their effects are never predictable.4 The work of Foulkes and others follows a hermeneutic orientation, wherein language functions mimetically in the representation of referents whose existence is assumed. For the most part deconstruction, which suspends the question of the referent, has been absent from discussions of propaganda, though its capacity to limit the term may be just as weak.5 [End Page 1] If propaganda has become largely synonymous with persuasion, we may still be able to distinguish it from art, if only provisionally, by examining specific cases where art has responded to the request to serve a political agenda. Since Plato, artists in the West have practiced their calling in full knowledge of its potential for conflicting with or reinforcing political ideologies. But while art's struggle for independence from propaganda has been allegorized—famously, for example, in dystopian novels like Orwell's 1984—criticism of those works, too, often fails to articulate any clear line of demarcation between them.6 Most commentators agree that the adaptation of art to serve propagandistic purposes would be a form of aesthetic suicide. There have been few studies, however, of artworks as self-conscious responses to requests to make propaganda. W.B. Yeats's "On Being Asked for a War Poem" (originally prompted by a request from Henry James to write about World War I) and Alfred Hitchcock's two wartime shorts, Bon Voyage and Aventure Malgache, produced in 1944 at the behest of the British Ministry of Information, are notable contributions to this conversation. It is as if each work is a speech act—not to be read constatively, as propaganda, but as a dramatization of art's response to being asked for it.7 On the one hand, each declares independence from political causes, so they can be read as traditional statements of artistic idealism or intellectual freedom. On the other hand, in keeping with Paul de Man's warnings to criticism, they also imply the impossibility of sustaining their passing affirmations of art's integrity; as a result, their provisional distinction between art and propaganda is eventually undone, leaving the terms synonymous and indeterminable, after all. Yeats's poem and Hitchcock's films may be best seen as forms of temporary "meddling" (Yeats's word), or ostensibly apolitical interventions whose brief evocations of art's demystifying potential last only so long as they are read. Let's look first at Yeats's poem: [End Page 2] "On Being Asked for a War Poem" I think it better that in times like theseA poet's mouth be silent, for in truthWe have no gift to set a statesman right;He has had enough of meddling that can pleaseA young girl in the indolence of her youth,Or an old man upon a winter's night.8 Yeats's seeming preference for silence on being asked for a war poem is actually misdirection, since it's quickly belied by the crystalline articulation that follows, which gives readers the reason for his refusal to take a side.
