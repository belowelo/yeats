## Abstract

This chapter looks at how Yeats's later writings are inundated with names. This usage transcends mere everyday nominalism. Instead, it implies a creative and idiosyncratic form of aesthetic shorthand. Yeats was writing his poems and prose works of such promiscuous naming at a time when the philosophical problems posed by naming were being posed in the analytical philosophy of Bertrand Russell and G. E. Moore. Yeats's own responses to such ideas are traced via his correspondence with Moore's brother, the poet and artist Thomas Sturge Moore. A sense of the wider nature of Yeats's philosophical reading and thinking at this time and its relationship to his own writing is also recuperated. The chapter argues that whether it is in referring to Nietzsche, Schopenhauer, Noh theatre, Plotinus or Shelley, the proper name for Yeats becomes evidence of the created image producing its own object of knowledge.
