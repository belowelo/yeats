## Abstract

Michael Cavanagh Tower and Boat: Yeats and Seamus Heaney In 1988, Seamus Heaney worked in the Reading Room of the National Library in Dublin on a selection of, and introduction to, Yeats's poems for the Field Day Anthology ofIrish Writing. By his account, it was a period of intense labor: "I had six weeks of a battened-down sense of being nose-to-the grindstone," he recalls, but it was out of this labor, "with a sense of the weight lifted;' that the short, uniform, buoyant, and brilliant poems called "Squarings" were born, which form the central part of his 1991 collection Seeing Things. 1 During that labor Heaney probably walked across the courtyard on Kildare Street to the National Museum to view, in the north room of the ground floor, the 2000 yearold Gold Boat from the Broighter Hoard from County Derry, a replica ofwhat must have been a heavy-duty, hard-working Iron Age vessel. The Gold Boat is, however, anything but heavy-looking: six inches long, humble in design but luminescent , with seven needle-slender golden oars on both sides, and a toy-like mast, it is-as Yeats might have written-"delicate as an eyelid."2 The Gold Boat appears on the front of the American edition of Seeing Things; boats are a prominent image in that collection, and Heaney has described "Squarings" as the product of his ability to get "into little coffers of pastness."3 As a work of art as well as a "coffer of pastness" detached from its immediate historical context , the boat represents a kind of counteragent to history. As such, its appeal to Heaney may be associated with Yeats, who is the master spirit of Seeing Things and the dominant influence in Heaney's career after the mid-198os. In April, 1999, much was made, in the press and on television in Ireland, of Heaney's sixtieth birthday. Yeats's name came up often, but usually in the claim that Heaney had attained a stature comparable to Yeats, not that he was a similar poet or had somehow assumed Yeats's position. Their temperaments and stylesYeats 's presumptive and outspoken, Heaney's tentative and tactful-also set them far apart. Yeats's political views were antidemocratic and extreme; Heaney's are tolerant and moderate. On one occasion, when Heaney was asked "How do you react when people speak ofyou as the National Bard, slipping into the Yeatsean overcoat?" he dismissed the comparison as" blurb-speak" and insisted there was no writer in Ireland and perhaps not in the English-speaking world who was like Yeats, that is "with a comparable mission, with a career going forward on a theatrical front, on a poetic front, on, as it were, a national cultural front."4 Heaney's answer is understandable; one can hardly imagine him having the presumption to accept the honor suggested, even if he believed it were true and had worked toward its accomplishment. Nevertheless, in spite oftheir differences, Heaney andYeats have a great deal in common. Their commonalities have received surprisingly little attention. They stem, no doubt, from affinity, but also from some conscious and, perhaps, semiconscious self-modeling on Heaney's part. Many aspects ofYeats's career may be seen in Heaney's.
