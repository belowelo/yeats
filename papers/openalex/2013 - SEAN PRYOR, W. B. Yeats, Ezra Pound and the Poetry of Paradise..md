## Abstract

SEAN PRYOR's study on 'the poetry of paradise' arranges its thesis according to two defining questions: 'what kind of poetry is paradise?' and conversely, 'what kind of paradise is poetry'? (3). Pryor doesn't shy away from the complexity of these questions—he acknowledges that 'the quest to deliver paradise is … paradoxical … at once self-defeating and self-fulfilling' (3)—yet the great critical sophistication of his study as well as its occasional obscurity are both consequences of this complexity. Pryor's thesis positions Yeats and Pound in relation to a history of paradisiacal representations and a conceptual contrast between the poet as seer (vates) and poet as maker (poet). Pryor traces the use of the term 'paradise' and its oscillation between heavenly and earthly referents from the earthly root of paradise as an 'enclosure', often used to refer to the gardens of early Persian satraps, to its role within English literary language from Sidney in the sixteenth century to Shelley in the nineteenth century. The idea prevalent in this period, of restoring a 'lost' paradise through poetry, was implicitly criticized by Romanticism's fascination with ruins and fragmentary poetry. Modernism was also fascinated by the way that poets are forced to create 'out of the wrecks of earlier Edens' (43) but tended to espouse the idea that beautiful, fragmentary ruins are closer to paradise than whole poetic structures. The impossibility, then, of restoring paradise through poetry diminished the role of poet as maker (poet), deliverer or producer of paradise, just as it renewed the role of poet as visionary seer (vates). For Yeats and Pound as seers, 'paradise is an object beyond poetry, an other which a poem may indicate or copy' (110), but as makers, 'paradise is more poetry' (110). Yeats and Pound occupy both roles at different points and Pryor is interested in the moments in their poetry when 'the opposition of the vates and the maker collapses' (98).
