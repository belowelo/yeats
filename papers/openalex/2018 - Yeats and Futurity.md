## Abstract

POETRY, SAID D. H. LAWRENCE, tends either to be 'the voice of the far future, exquisite and ethereal', or to be 'the voice of the past, rich, magnificent'.1 Lawrence wanted to write the poetry of the present, to capture the fleeting moment in a supple, responsive free verse. Yet the free verse of other modernist poets – Mina Loy or William Carlos Williams, for example – does not often or primarily aspire to capture an evanescent present. And nothing seems further from Lawrence's ambition than the supple stanzas of ottava rima in which W. B. Yeats famously imagines a voice of past, present, and future: a bird singing to lords and ladies in Byzantium of what is past or passing or to come. But Lawrence's remark raises a good question: in which voice do Yeats's own poems sing? A customary answer, which Yeats sometimes encouraged, is that he sings in the voice of the past, however rich and magnificent, and that this separates him from his younger contemporaries. 'I must choose a traditional stanza', he declares in defiance of new-fangled verse-forms, for 'Ancient salt is best packing'.2 But it was a matter of more than stanzas; it involved an entire poetics, a celebration of and longing for a lost past. In A Portrait of the Artist as a Young Man, Stephen Dedalus recalls lines from 'He remembers Forgotten Beauty' (1896): 'When my arms wrap you round I press / My heart upon the loveliness / That has long faded from the world'.3 'Not this', thinks Stephen, 'Not at all. I desire to press in my arms the loveliness which has not yet come into the world'.4 Stephen, wanting the poetry of the future, relegates Yeats to being a poet of the past.
