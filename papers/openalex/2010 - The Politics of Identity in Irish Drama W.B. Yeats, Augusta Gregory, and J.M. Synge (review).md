## Abstract

Reviewed by: The Politics of Identity in Irish Drama: W.B. Yeats, Augusta Gregory, and J.M. Synge Mary Trotter George Cusack . The Politics of Identity in Irish Drama: W.B. Yeats, Augusta Gregory, and J.M. Synge. New York: Routledge, 2009. Pp. 197. $103.00 (Hb). George Cusack's book, part of Routledge's Literary Criticism and Cultural Theory Series, analyses how William Butler Yeats, Augusta Gregory, and John Millington Synge explored their equivocal positions as both Anglo-Irish and nationalist artists through their dramas. All of these playwrights used their plays to challenge what they perceived as the Celtic Revival's imaginings of Irish homogeneity by exposing gender, class, or ideological conflicts within Irish culture. Cusack claims that while Yeats's, Gregory's, and Synge's particular dramas did not establish "the dynamic and self-aware national identity that their authors desired, . . . the conflict between nationality and individual subjectivity that their work illuminates nonetheless became a part of the Irish cultural landscape" (172). In the text's introduction, Cusack acknowledges that his premise is not new, but he states that his approach differs from recent cultural histories of Irish drama by focusing on textual over societal concerns and on how performance became a site of cultural negotiation within the Irish dramatic movement. He leans heavily on cultural theorists Seamus Deane, David Lloyd, and Terry Eagleton for his ideological contexts, but most of his text consists of close readings of well-known works by Yeats, Gregory, and Synge. In chapter one, Cusack comes closest to melding textual critique and performance history with a close reading of Yeats and Gregory's play Kathleen ni Houlihan, claiming rightly that in performance the ideologies of the performers and the audience transformed the more ambiguous [End Page 268] politics of the text. The following chapter considers two of Yeats's earliest plays - The Countess Cathleen and The King's Threshold - as discussions of the artist's role in society. Cusack argues that in the first decade of Yeats's involvement in the national theatre he grew increasingly antagonistic towards what he saw as an overly populist national aesthetic within the Irish cultural revival; this aesthetic, he felt, undermined the personal vision and authority of the poet/playwright. Chapter three interprets Gregory's use of language and gender to express her own identity position in Irish politics, and it offers a strong reading of her play Devorgilla, among other texts. Chapters four and five turn to Synge's plays, claiming that Synge saw in language a tool for the individual to escape from the suffocating pressure toward conformity found in many communities - including the nationalist movement. Chapter five culminates in a discussion of the controversy surrounding The Playboy of the Western World and claims that Synge's pointed attack on conservative nationalist platitudes about the rural West led to the Abbey directors' ultimate alienation from the movement. While it is an overstatement to say that the end of these playwrights' influence as national dramatists came in 1907 - Yeats and Gregory continued careers as playwrights and managers decades after 1907, and Synge's plays continued to be produced at the Abbey and across Ireland - Cusack's point that Yeats's, Gregory's, and Synge's first decade as self-described Irish playwrights was one of political identity formation and negotiation is well taken, and it is useful to observe those developments reflected in their plays. Cusack's readings, however, do not often acknowledge alternative interpretations of the dramas or the political and artistic events around their first performances. For instance, he claims that Yeats changed his 1903 play The King's Threshold in 1922 from a tragicomedy (in which a king acquiesces to a poet's hunger strike) to a tragedy (in which the poet dies on a hunger strike) because Yeats had grown cynical about art and politics. Another likely and widely discussed reason for the change in the play, however, was the death of Cork mayor and nationalist activist Terence MacSwiney while on a hunger strike in a London prison in 1920.
