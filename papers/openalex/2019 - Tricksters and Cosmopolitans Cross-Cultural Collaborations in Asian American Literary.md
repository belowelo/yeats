## Abstract

Tricksters and Cosmopolitans and East-West Literary Imagination reflect on obscured cross-cultural connections in the histories and geographies of literary productions that span the globe. Both Magosaki and Hakutani identify and mediate those links, which have been rendered illegible by academic and disciplinary or geographical and linguistic divisions. Their signal achievements are to challenge us to reconceptualize the way we think about literary traditions by reading and writing across fields.Tricksters and Cosmopolitans is a unique literary-historical study that attempts to conceive connections between literary analysis and the study of book history and between theories in African American and Asian American literary criticisms. In her opening methodological remarks, Magosaki proposes to approach "the production of the Asian cosmopolitan subject" (1) with a liberal yet subversive application of "Signifying" (3). According to Henry Louis Gates Jr.'s seminal book The Signifying Monkey (1988), the theory of Signifying is derived from a discrete black tradition that dates back to the divine trickster figure of Yoruba mythology in Africa, which transmuted itself into its African American descendant, the Signifying Monkey. In common parlance, The Signifying Monkey theorizes the "blackness" of African American literature, or Signifying in the black literary tradition, in which black writers Signify on each other. Tricksters and Cosmopolitans, which transgresses the postulated borders, mediates the two ethnic minorities' traditions and suggests that Asian American writers, such as Sui Sin Far, use the art of silence as Signifying.One of the book's interesting cross-cultural insights emerges in its discussion, in chapter 1, of a genealogical analogy between the emergence of African American and Asian American literature. It focuses on Charles W. Chesnutt and Sui Sin Far, the first African American and Asian American authors, respectively, to be published by major literary publishing houses in the United States. Particularly illuminating are the coterminous juxtapositions between Chesnutt and Sui Sin Far and between Gates's The Signifying Monkey and John K. Young's Black Writers, White Publishers (2006)—this is a critical move with perhaps a nod to Joseph Rezek's essay "Print, Writing, and the Difference Media Make: Revisiting The Signifying Monkey after Book History" (2015), which draws our attention to the often subsumed difference between writing and print that brings the former into the marketplace. However, Magosaki is less interested in stressing this difference than in rethinking the relationality between them, and her analysis is presented ingeniously by way of discussing the relationality between contemporary Asian American writers and white editors/publishers who occasionally are themselves poets and writers, which could result in "extraordinary cross-cultural collaboration" (40).In the remaining chapters, the central (though unarticulated) trope in the book shifts from genealogies to geographies that make possible the emergence of "the new cosmopolitan Asian American subject" (40): Magosaki examines San Francisco in chapter 2 and LA–Paris–New York in chapter 3. Each chapter pairs two projects, namely, close readings of select books and historical examinations of the material production of those books by publishing houses. The titles of all the texts that Magosaki chooses to discuss contain (incidentally, or not so incidentally) food: Jessica Hagedorn's Pet Food and Tropical Apparitions (1975), Karen Tei Yamashita's Tropic of Orange (1997), Monique Truong's The Book of Salt (2003), and Min Jin Lee's Free Food for Millionaires (2007). Considered as a group, these texts reveal the emergent structural paradox of global capitalism as a condition for the dream of the Asian cosmopolitan subject—a condition that Magosaki's superb, nuanced reading suggests involves the burial of the more complex currents in global inequalities, in particular the North-South divide, which food, both material and metaphorical, often gestures toward: minorities eclipsed in multiculturalism, migrants from the global South, toiling bodies of colonial laborers with "[sweat] salt rendered imperceptible" (111), and an inescapable web of credit card debt in which the Asian cosmopolitan subject is ensnared and exploited as a consumer.Whereas Magosaki attends to the North-South divide as defined in economic terms, Hakutani places a much greater emphasis on the older twofold partition that is cultural, East versus West, which is discussed in East-West Literary Imagination, significantly, as literarily contingent. As the subtitle signals, the goal of Hakutani's book is to reread authors from different literary traditions (W. B. Yeats, Yone Noguchi, Ezra Pound, Richard Wright, Jack Kerouac, Toni Morrison, Sonia Sanchez, James Emanuel, and others) and to trace what he terms "East-West cross-culturalism" (13).On first reading, the book appears to be traditional; the conventional literary groupings serve as the framework of its organization, with thirteen chapters that are divided into three parts, "transcendentalists," "modernists," and "African American writers." However, reading across the chapters reveals a novel design that is ambitious in its range, as it considers literary genealogies—in particular, of forms and genres—that crisscross the putative lines of national literary histories. For instance, considering together chapters 5 (Noguchi and Pound), 7 (Wright), 8 (Kerouac), and 13 (Wright, Sanchez, and Emanuel) offers the reader a fascinating picture of the haiku, a Japanese poetry form of seventeen syllables, which had a significant bearing on imagism, jazz, the Beat generation, and beyond. Hakutani argues, "The most influential East-West artistic, cultural, and literary exchange that has taken place in modern and postmodern times was reading and writing of haiku in the West" (241), and he puts Richard Wright at center stage in this literary genealogy.Hakutani's book critiques and questions the linear progression of national literary traditions by using East-West evolution as both metaphor and model. Unlike Franco Moretti's evolutionary model of European literature, which arises from a European space conceptualized as an archipelago, the "heuristic" approach (23) taken in East-West Literary Imagination is not structured around connections between literature and space but between literatures mediated by translators such as R. H. Blyth, who was interned as an enemy alien in Japan during World War II. "Unlike European literatures," Hakutani argues, "American literature has become a hybridization of Eastern and Western literatures" (3). Thus, a new type of analysis is required that allows for a focus on East-West exchange, wherein literary cultures "evolve" (23), and that reckons texts "not as part of a national literature, but as an index to an evolving literature of cultural exchanges" (3).Although East-West Literary Imagination is not a theory-driven work, a more robust conceptual framework would help account for the way that the genre of haiku moved around the globe. Likewise, Tricksters and Cosmopolitans has left its turn to black literary theory—and the "template" (6, 17) for its analysis of the cosmopolitan Asian American subject—relatively undertheorized. However, even with these hesitations, both books should be applauded for their achievements, which promise to inspire further reconsiderations of literary traditions.
