## Abstract

Reviewed by: Deep Rooted Things: Empire and Nation in the Poetry and Drama of William Butler Yeats Heather L. Clark Deep Rooted Things: Empire and Nation in the Poetry and Drama of William Butler Yeats, by Rob Doggett , pp. 192. Notre Dame: University of Notre Dame Press, $25. Deep Rooted Things is a welcome addition to recent postcolonial criticism that seeks to defend Yeats from accusations of colonial elitism and Ascendancy nostalgia. Building upon the work of Jahan Ramazani, Edward Said, Marjorie Howes, and others, Rob Doggett situates himself within what he calls the "second wave of postcolonial Yeats criticism," which "presents a liberationist Yeats." These postcolonial critics argue with Seamus Deane's recasting of Yeats, in the [End Page 150] 1980s and 1990s, as a quasi-imperialist who shared more affinities with the colonizer than the colonized. While Doggett does not let Yeats off the hook entirely, he strives to present a more nuanced version of the poet—one that not only emphasizes Yeats's hybridity, but also his fluidity. Doggett's Yeats is postmodern, continually in transition, never showing his hand, and never offering closure. He is a poet whose work "folds back upon itself, continually exposing and undercutting its own dialectical drive toward totalization, precisely because it explicitly refuses escapism even as it explicitly desires it." This Yeats is closer to James Joyce than to T. S. Eliot, and closer to Paul Muldoon than to Seamus Heaney. Throughout Deep Rooted Things, Doggett emphasizes that Yeats's work is about "chaos, uncertainty, and incoherence"; this is a far cry from the self-assured, sometimes arrogant Yeats of Deane's Celtic Revivals. Doggett focuses upon Yeats's sense of uncertainty about the emerging nation in order to show that the poet had a much more complex relationship with nationalism than many critics—even postcolonial ones—have assumed. Like many before him, he sees a Yeats who was deeply pained by the increasingly violent face of republicanism, yet he argues that the poet did not simply regress into escapist fantasies in which his main aim was to "Sing the peasantry, and then / Hard-riding country gentlemen." Rather, Yeats sought to offer a new version of the burgeoning nation that would strike a balance between Ireland's insular romanticism and its European modernity—an Ireland that looked backward and forward, inward and outward in the same moment. According to Doggett, however, Yeats never quite captured this "ambiguous" historiographic moment. As a result, his political poems must be read as open rather than closed, uneasy rather than confident. While Doggett shows a clear command of postcolonial theory, he is at his strongest when he defends his thesis through close readings of the poems, rather than through extended theoretical discussions. When he takes the latter approach, his arguments are sometimes obscured by jargon and repetition; the phrase "In other words . . ." appears frequently after long theoretical explanations. In Chapter 2, which deals with performances of gender and nationhood in Cathleen ni Houlihan and Deirdre, Doggett relies too much on feminist, performance, and postcolonial theory to explain Yeats's subversive attack on traditional nationalism—a relatively clear idea that becomes progressively murkier as more layers of theory are applied. However, his reading of Cathleen ni Houlihan as "fundamentally conflicted" in its loyalties to the nationalist cause deserves attention. The third chapter offers close readings of "Nineteen Hundred and Nineteen" and "Meditations in Time of Civil War," in which the poet looks back upon his former naive idealism and laments the present state of violence. While there are some interesting insights here, Doggett's overall claim— [End Page 151] that in these poems Yeats "emphasizes violence as rupture, moments to be understood, not as the culmination of a series of past events"—echoes readings offered by others, particularly Seamus Heaney. The two final chapters are the strongest in the book. Chapter 4 deals with the theme of imaginative exile in Yeats's work, a topic that Doggett rightly notes has been overlooked by critics.
