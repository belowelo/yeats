## Abstract

Literature read theologically, and by a master in a range of fields and disciplines, from comparative theology, especially Buddhist-Christian dialogue, to phenomenology, can be both a daunting and deeply illuminating task.In his kinetic and acute Foreword to Joysis Crisis, Joseph O'Leary's playful, provocative, and (for me) more accessible of the two works under review here, Mark Patrick Hederman opens: "Dear reader, I stand outside the doorway of this enormously daunting volume begging you to take your courage in your hands and step inside" (p.vii).When a writer can linguistically and philosophically dance around and with Joyce, confident of his own voice, then readers, even if sometimes taken on a "rough ride" (p.x), as Hederman writes, will gain insights and connections otherwise hidden and unthought.O'Leary's subtitle here, and his employment of the word "Theomasochistically" hints at both the level, depth, and wordplays ahead.It dredges up the once pervasive sexual repression of Ireland (a theme taken up again in the second volume), while the Finnegans Wake-like puns of the main title, Joysis Crisis, tease and chide and also counter and balance the negative with ebullience and laughter (p.31).Indeed, for O'Leary, the life and writings of Joyce provide a well (if not ocean) for theologians in a time of spiritual barrenness and confusion.He writes: "Theology needs to find an understanding of the divine large enough to embrace the multifarious life of the modern city, including all its dimensions of chance and contingency, and large enough to embrace the complexity which Joyce, to an unprecedented degree, revealed in ordinary human beings" (p.359).This embrace is an acceptance of the contradictions, ethical strivings, moral failures, transparent ugliness, sombre beauty, and riotous yearnings that characterise humanity, but which are too often denied or simply condemned, especially within the Catholic Ireland of O'Leary's youth.O'Leary is challenging us (and the Church) to pay more heed to "the broken and fragmented apprehension of divinity amid the complexity of modern life and languages" (p.361).For O'Leary, moreover, "James Joyce suffered from God" (p.1), which both anticipates Joyce's rebukes and mocking of the Catholic Ireland he could never successfully flee, but also reveals his lifelong battle with the divine, a wrestling that may not have ended up in a wounded faith (characteristic of some post-Shoah Jewish theologians), but a relationship nonetheless.Joysis Crisis is structured chronologically through Joyce's major
