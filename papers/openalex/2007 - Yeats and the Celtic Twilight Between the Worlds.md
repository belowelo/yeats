## Abstract

Like Voltaire's proclamation that if God did not exist it would be necessary to invent him, so too is the case with history: if the past is not recorded, it becomes necessary for a culture to partake in a narrative that invents (or re-invents) it. In this way, history becomes am imagined one, woven into the tapestry of the culture through storytelling and oral tradition and fortified by subsequent generations who re-tell, re-invent, re-imagine. This is the particular case of the Irish, who, faced with the absence of any substantive written history of their Celtic past, resorted to the fertile ground of imagination from which to paint the blank canvas of the past. In Yeats' poem Fight with the Sea the Celtic Irish hero Cuchulain, so prominent in Celtic oral tradition, leaves his wife Emer and goes off with a younger woman; Emer trains their son to be a hero to one day avenge her right and appease her anger, knowing full well that when and their son confront one another, the father will slay the However, this knowledge of paternity remains a secret until the moment, toward the end of the poem, when the son proclaims, Cuchulain I, mighty Cuchulain's son. At this juncture of the poem, this suddenly revealed paternity changes me power dynamic, now favoring the son and indicating a legacy to which must inevitably concede. Emer is a woman scorned, a woman who in the end possesses that which will destroy the scorning father. It is that unknown entity, that which cannot be named, which does the destroying: Cuchulain stirred/Stared on the horses of the sea, and heard/The cars of battle and his own name cried;/And fought with the invulnerable tide (Yeats 36). For the once Celtic hero, the sea becomes the overwhelming force which he can never defeat and upon which he engenders the eternal struggle between what is past and what is present, a fusion into a twilight between them. becomes an ancient hero transformed by a poet's vision: Yeats' is formed in a place between the ancient folklore drawn from a Celtic past and his own place in modern literature as a distinct poet with a distinct voice. As Declan Kiberd points out, this poem, along with many of his early works, rework traditional themes and images as if redeemed by Yeatsian style (306). Yeats' collection of folk-tales in Celtic Twilight certainly bear this out, as their informal fragmentary structure reads more like something one would hear if a tape recorder were left on and one roamed the Irish countryside in search of random folk tales; many of these tales, so steeped in oral tradition, told of the supernatural, stories of banshees and fairies, demons and curses, village ghosts and mystic poets. Indeed this reflected the nature of the Irish peasantry, who subsisted on these stories almost as necessity. As Yeats notes at the beginning of one of these tales, 'Village Ghosts': The ancient map-makers wrote across unexplored regions, 'Here are lions.' Across the villages of fishermen and turners of the earth, so different are these from us, we can write but one line that is certain, 'Here are ghosts' (Yeats Mythologies 15). From an existence almost entirely devoid of the material, it becomes necessary for the poor people of Ireland to defer to their imaginations; their reliance on the wholly unreal becomes as essential as food and water. As Yeats observes in his poem He Wishes for the Cloths of Heaven: But I, being poor, have only my dreams;/I have spread my under your feet;/Tread softly because you tread on my dreams (Yeats 73). interest for Yeats in the Irish poor becomes twofold: their reliance on imagination and the unconsious in their everyday lives, and the importance of sharing their imagined histories.
