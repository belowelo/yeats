## Abstract

In the Irish Republic following the Anglo-Irish War, the figure of James Connolly was assimilated into the nation state's conservative nationalism. Poetic and balladic treatments of Connolly's spectacular martyrdom were central to the process by which the Irish working class was detached from its own interests, something for which Connolly himself has been blamed. W.B. Yeats's 'Easter, 1916' was central both to Connolly's canonization and to the class politics it perpetuated. The poem established Connolly as a potent but silenced icon, fusing him in death with the conservative nationalism he opposed in life. In 'Easter, 1916' socialism, feminism, and sexual liberation, shameful reminders of slurs such as endemic poverty, effeminacy and sexual incontinence with which the Irish had been stigmatized, were banished from a newly hegemonic bourgeois nationalism. This purging of social issues deemed embarrassingly personal was enabled through the promotion of martyred males like Connolly as screen figures obscuring class, gender, and sexual difference within the post-Rising nation. Yeats's poem rendered Connolly as a spectacular corpse divorced from the corpus of his political writings and activities prior to the Rising. In this essay I examine the challenges that Yeats's condensed construction of Connolly and the Rising poses to authors seeking to reimagine the relationship of nationalism to socialism. In At Swim, Two Boys, Jamie O'Neill seeks to 'change the subject' of Irish nationalism, disentangling Connolly and dissenting nationalist perspectives from the web of representation in which Yeats ensnared them.
