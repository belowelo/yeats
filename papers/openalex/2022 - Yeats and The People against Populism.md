## Abstract

Abstract This chapter follows the complex rhetorical gymnastics W.B. Yeats performed as he tried to balance a populist commitment to national revival with his attraction to the continental avant-garde. An early champion of those he thought of as The People, he argued throughout the 1890s that poets must return to the Irish-speaking cottagers of the West for cultural inspiration. While his enthusiasm for cultural nationalism wanes, a populist lens reveals Yeats's unique positioning in his middle period: imbibing Nietzsche and Ibsen encourages a valorization of the avant-gardist against the masses, yet he stubbornly advances The People as a positive source in Irish culture. (Conversely, James Joyce, equally enthusiastic about Ibsen, proudly bears the mantle Enemy of the People, his hostility evident in his 1901 essay "Day of the Rabblement.") Yeats's middle period desires to be Enemy and Friend of The People simultaneously, and his essays and poetry isolate The People from populism, which he comes to see as a purely middle-class affair. The Playboy riots and Hugh Lane gallery controversy put further pressure on Yeats's commitments to The People, and by 1913 Yeats comes to see them not as quaint cottagers but rather middle-class Catholics, and even newspaper magnate William Martin Murphy, whose spirit hovers over the poetry of this period, and who is "The People" in several poems. This chapter also explores Maud Gonne's writings, which repeatedly stage the close relationship between herself and the audience that she always called "The People."
