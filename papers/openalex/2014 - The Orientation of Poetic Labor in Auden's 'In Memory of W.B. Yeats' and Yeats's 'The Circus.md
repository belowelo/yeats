## Abstract

SCHOLARS have noted intriguing echoes of Yeats's language in Auden's poetry, and in a few cases they have discovered pointed allusions to specific poems.1 Joost Daalder suggests that this approach holds further potential: '[W]e may … not have found everything that there is to find.'2 Following Daalder's hint, this present investigation of Auden's 'In Memory of W.B. Yeats' (a meditation on poets, poetry, and poetic work written one month after Yeats's death) reveals an especially striking imitation of Yeats's style and thought. Portraying Yeats as a master labourer who writes poetry for and in a troubled world, Auden suggests that the centrepiece of Yeats's legacy is his wisdom about poetic labour. Emphasizing the physical nature of that poetic labour, Auden harks back to the ancient conception of the poet (in Latin, poeta) as maker.3 Scholars have found aspects of Yeats's style in Auden's elegy and have even indicated the possibility of allusions,4 Samuel Hynes naming it 'the most Yeatsian of Auden's poems';5 nevertheless, no one has yet explored the elegy's compelling resonance with 'The Circus Animals' Desertion,' a connection that draws out the idea of poetic labour as embodied art. Alluding to Yeats's poem, particularly at the end of 'In Memory of W.B. Yeats', Auden depicts poetic work as a journey down into the physical life (a metaphorical death suggested by the literal death of Yeats) followed by an ascent toward spiritual vision.
